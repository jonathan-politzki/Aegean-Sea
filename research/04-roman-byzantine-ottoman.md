## 1. The Roman Aegean (31 BC – c. AD 330)

### 1.1 Garum, salsamenta and the question of Aegean cetariae

**What garum was, in Pliny's words.** Pliny (NH 31.43, Bostock–Riley translation): "Another liquid, too, of a very exquisite nature, is that known as 'garum:' it is prepared from the intestines of fish and various parts which would otherwise be thrown away, macerated in salt; so that it is, in fact, the result of their putrefaction … At the present day, however, the most esteemed kind of garum is that prepared from the scomber, in the fisheries of Carthago Spartaria: it is known as 'garum of the allies,' and for a couple of congii we have to pay but little less than one thousand sesterces … For the production of garum, Clazomenæ is also famed, Pompeii, too, and Leptis; while for their muria, Antipolis, Thurii, and of late, Dalmatia, enjoy a high reputation." [http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=31:chapter=43, HIGH]

- The only Aegean-region garum centre Pliny names is **Clazomenae** (Ionian coast, Gulf of Smyrna). [same, HIGH]

**A Greek-language garum recipe survives** in the 10th-century *Geoponica* (Bk 20, ch. 46), which preserves earlier lore: "What is called liquamen is thus made: the intestines of fish are thrown into a vessel, and are salted; and small fish, especially atherinæ, or small mullets, or mænæ, or lycostomi, or any small fish, are all salted in the same manner; and they are seasoned in the sun, and frequently turned … A small basket of close texture is laid in the vessel filled with the small fish already mentioned, and the garum will flow into the basket … and the remainder of the feculence is made into alec. But the Bithynians prepare it in this manner: they indeed take small, or large mænæ … or scombri … and having applied two Italian sextarii of salt to a modius of the fish … they set this in the sun during two or three months, stirring them with a stick … But the best garum, which is called aimation, is thus made: the intestines of the tunny, with the gills, and the ichor, and the blood, are taken, and they are sprinkled with a sufficient quantity of salt; and they are left in the vessel during two months." (Owen's 1805 translation, vol. 2, pp. 299–301) [https://archive.org/details/b22041072_0002, HIGH]

**Was there commercial salt-fish production in the Aegean?** The academic picture is cautious:

- Ephraim Lytle, "The Economics of Saltfish Production in the Aegean During the Classical and Hellenistic Periods," *Journal of Maritime Archaeology* 13 (2018) 407–418, argues that "despite a dearth of literary and archaeological evidence for the commercial production of salted fish or fish sauces in the Aegean … it has been argued … that such production must have been common. This paper suggests those arguments are probably wrong," while noting that Black Sea salt-fish was produced without permanent vats and shipped in baskets, so absence of vats is not proof of absence. [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11457-018-9207-1, HIGH (abstract)]
- Dimitra Mylona, "To Salt or Not to Salt: A Review of Evidence for Processed Marine Products and Local Traditions in the Aegean Through Time," *JMA* 13 (2018), reviews the same problem across periods. [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11457-018-9216-0, MEDIUM (title/venue verified; article paywalled)]
- A paper titled "Salting the East: evidence for salted fish and fish-products from the Aegean Sea in Roman times" exists on Academia.edu; I could not open it. [https://www.academia.edu/17654568/, LOW]
- Kyzikos (Propontis) is a well-studied case: watchtowers for tuna, public fishing grounds and saltpans from which the city took fees, a fishermen's guild attested epigraphically in the Roman period—yet "there are no traces of salting factories or salted fish amphorae in Kyzikos." (H. Çoruh Kurt, "Fishing and Tuna Trade in Kyzikos," *Oannes* 7/1, 2025) [https://dergipark.org.tr, DOI 10.33469/oannes.1611585, HIGH]

**Bottom line:** No excavated Roman *cetaria* (salting-vat complex) in the Aegean islands or Greece proper turned up in this research; the well-known vat complexes are in Iberia, North Africa, Italy and the Black Sea. Aegean salt-fish almost certainly existed (Galen and Athenaeus discuss *tarichos* at length – see §1.5) but was probably made without permanent stone vats. Rate this synthesis MEDIUM.

**Roman fish tanks on Crete (piscinae, not salting vats).** N. D. Mourtzas, "Fish tanks of eastern Crete (Greece) as indicators of the Roman sea level," *J. Archaeological Science* 39 (2012), and his companion paper on the Gulf of Matala: rock-cut Roman fish tanks at **Matala, Chersonissos and Mochlos**, plus a fish trap at Zakros; the "tanks" at Sitia are re-identified as quarries. The tanks are of Roman date (2nd c. AD is the usual attribution) and now lie roughly 1–1.5 m under water, which is why they are used as sea-level markers. [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440312000702 and https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440311003748, MEDIUM (abstracts via search; full text paywalled)] The British School at Athens also published "Rock-cut Fish Tanks in Eastern Crete." [https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-of-the-british-school-at-athens/article/abs/rockcut-fish-tanks-in-eastern-crete/E51BC05E73EB388D8A21A11EF0848CA1, MEDIUM]

**The Bosporus bonito fishery, as the Romans inherited it.** Strabo 7.6.2 (Loeb, LacusCurtius): the pelamydes "are hatched in the marshes of Lake Maeotis, and when they have gained a little strength they rush out through the mouth of the lake in schools and move along the Asian shore as far as Trapezus and Pharnacia … the creatures take such fright at a certain white rock which projects from the Chalcedonian shore that they forthwith turn to the opposite shore … Because of the narrowness of the area, they are even caught by hand … and thus afford the Byzantines and the Roman people considerable revenue." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/strabo/7f*.html, HIGH]

### 1.2 Oppian's *Halieutica*

**Author, date, form.** Greek didactic poem of 3,606 hexameters in five books by Oppian of Cilicia, dedicated to the co-emperors Marcus Aurelius and Commodus (joint reign 176–180). A legend, reported by the ancient *Lives*, says Marcus paid one gold coin per line—hence the nickname "golden verses"; treat the anecdote as legend. Over 70 Byzantine manuscripts survive. (Krystyna Bartol, *Antigone*, Dec 2022) [https://antigonejournal.com/2022/12/oppian-halieutica/, MEDIUM] The poem addresses the emperor as "Antoninus." [Mair's Loeb translation on LacusCurtius, https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/1*.html, HIGH]

**Book 1** – Proem contrasts the fisherman's lot with the hunter's and fowler's: hunters have dogs, shelter, fresh water; fowlers can see their prey; but for fishermen "their labours are uncertain, and unstable as a dream is the hope that flatters their hearts." Then a catalogue of fish by habitat: sandy shallows (red mullet, sole, mackerel…), muddy bottoms (rays, turbot), weedy shores (maenis, atherine, smaris, bogue), rocks (perch, wrasses, saupe, moray, merou), the deep (hake), and the open sea (tunny, swordfish, pilot-fish); then eels, dolphins, crustaceans, molluscs and sea-monsters. "Infinite and beyond ken are the tribes that move and swim in the depth of the sea, and none could name them" (1.80–82). [Bk 1 at LacusCurtius, HIGH; the 1.80–82 quotation via Antigone, MEDIUM]

**Book 2** – The "law of the sea": "Among fishes neither justice is of any account nor is there any mercy nor love; for all the fish that swim are bitter foes to one another"; "The stronger ever devours the weaker" (2.43–47). Set pieces on the torpedo (electric ray), the angler-fish's lure, the octopus vs. moray, the crab that wedges a pebble in the oyster; only the grey mullet is held up as harmless. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/2*.html, HIGH]

**Book 3** – The virtues of the fisher and the four modes of fishing. The fisher must have "body and limbs both swift and strong," cunning, daring, temperance, keen sight, endurance of "wintry weather and the thirsty season of Sirius," and "must be fond of labour and must love the sea." Then: "Fourfold modes of hunting their prey in the sea have fishermen devised" – hooks (hand-lines, rods with horsehair, multi-hook rigs), nets (casting-nets, draw-nets, bag-nets, seines, cover-nets, ground-nets, ball-nets, trawls), weels/basket traps that bring "joy to their masters while they sleep at ease," and "the long pronged Trident." The **tunny hunt**: "a skilful Tunny-watcher ascends a steep high hill" and "remarks the various shoals, their kind and size"; the nets are "set forth in the waves like a city" with "its gate-warders and gates withal and inner courts," while the "Tunnies speed on in line, like ranks of men marching tribe by tribe." (Loeb pp. 349–353, 401) [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/3*.html, HIGH]

**Book 4** – Fish caught through their passions: parrot-wrasse males lured by a towed decoy female into weels; grey mullet following a dragged female; cuttlefish embracing a decoy; the sargue's "love" of goats, so that fishermen dress in goat-skins and horns; the merle-wrasse's jealousy; "such goads of passion urge them on." Also **night fishing by torch**: fishermen use a "lighted torch" so that fish "rush about the boat" and are struck with tridents; and poisoning with cyclamen root and clay smeared at fish lairs. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/4*.html, HIGH]

**Book 5** – Hunting the sea-monster/whale (kill its pilot-fish first; giant hooks baited with bull's liver; inflated skins on the line to exhaust the beast); a passionate condemnation of dolphin-hunting as impious, blaming the "overweening Thracians" (5.416, 5.519); the **sponge-cutters** (5.612–665): "A man is girt with a long rope above his waist and, using both hands, in one he grasps a heavy mass of lead and in his right hand he holds a sharp bill, while in the jaws of his mouth he keeps white oil" (625–627); he "spits out the oil" (634) to see; "hateful blood is sprinkled straightway from the Sponges" (651); "Often when the sponge-cutter has leapt into the deep waters of the sea … he comes up no more, unhappy man, having encountered some huge and hideous beast" (662–664). Closing prayer: "may thy ships be steered from harm, sped by gentle winds and fair; and always for thee may the sea teem with fish; and may Poseidon, Lord of Safety, guard and keep unshaken the nether foundations which hold the roots of Earth" (675–677). [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/5*.html, HIGH]

Modern study: Emily Kneebone, *Oppian's Halieutica: Charting a Didactic Epic* (Cambridge, 2020). [https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/oppians-halieutica/570A23145EA31B950D8AE82B2E4E90CB, MEDIUM]

### 1.3 Aelian, *On the Nature of Animals* (early 3rd c.)

Aelian was a Roman who wrote in Greek; his fishing anecdotes are second-hand but valuable. Scholfield's Loeb translation is at attalus.org.

- **The first artificial fly (NA 15.1).** "Between Beroea and Thessalonica there flows a river called the Astraeus … These flies settle on the stream … So when a fish observes a Hippurus on the surface it swims up noiselessly under water …" and the anglers, unable to use the real flies, "wrap the hook in scarlet wool, and to the wool they attach two feathers that grow beneath a cock's wattles and are the colour of wax. The fishing-rod is six feet long, and so is the line. So they let down this lure, and the fish attracted and excited by the colour, comes to meet it, and fancying from the beauty of the sight that he is going to have a wonderful banquet, opens wide his mouth, is entangled with the hook, and gains a bitter feast, for he is caught." [https://www.attalus.org/translate/animals15.html, HIGH] (Macedonia, not the Aegean, but the earliest description of fly-fishing anywhere.)
- **Tunny-watchers and lead-weighted nets (NA 15.5).** "the inhabitants of the whole of that country know exactly of the coming of the tunny … much gear has been got ready to deal with them, boats and nets and a high lookout-place … Two high pine-trunks held apart by wide balks of timber, are set up … Each of the boats has six young men, strong rowers, on either side. The nets are of considerable length; they are not too light and so far from being kept floating by corks are actually weighted with lead … the watcher, whose mysterious skill and naturally sharp sight enable him to see the fish, announces to the fishermen the quarter from which they are coming … like a general he gives the signal … And frequently he will tell the total number of fish and not be off the mark." [same, HIGH]
- **Night torch-fishing in Euboea, with dolphins (NA 2.8)** – the earliest explicit Aegean description of what Greeks now call *pyrofani*: "There are stories which reach us from Euboea of fisher-folk in those parts sharing their catch equally with the dolphins … The weather must be calm, and if it is, they attach to the prow of their boats some hollow braziers with fire burning in them, and one can see through them, so that while retaining the fire they do not conceal the light. They call them lanterns. Now the fish are afraid of the brightness and are dazzled by the glare … Of course in that condition it is perfectly easy to harpoon them. So when the dolphins observe that the fishermen have lit their fire, they get ready to act, and while the men row softly the dolphins scare the fish on the outskirts and push them and prevent any escape … And the fishermen loyally and gratefully resign to their comrades in the chase their just portion." [https://www.attalus.org/translate/animals2.html, HIGH]

### 1.4 Pliny, *Natural History* Book 9, and Roman fishponds vs. Greek practice

Pliny NH 9.79–81 (Rackham chapter numbering; = §§168–172):

- "Oyster ponds were first invented by Sergius Orata on the Gulf of Baiae, in the time of the orator Lucius Crassus, before the Marsic war; his motive was not greed but avarice, and he made a great profit out of his practical ingenuity." [https://www.attalus.org/pliny/hn9b.html, HIGH]
- "In the same period the elder Licinius Murena invented fishponds for all the other sorts of fish, and his example was subsequently followed by the celebrated achievements of Philippus and Hortensius. Lucullus had built a channel that cost more than a country house, by actually cutting through a mountain near Naples and letting in the sea; this was why Pompey the Great used to call him 'Xerxes in Roman dress.' After his decease the fish from this pond sold for 4,000,000 sestertii." [same, HIGH]
- "At Bauli in the Baiae district the orator Hortensius had a fishpond containing a lamprey which he fell so deeply in love with that he is believed to have wept when it expired." [same, HIGH]
- In the same book Pliny ranks regional fish and names "sturgeon at Rhodes" among the celebrated. [same, HIGH]

**Roman vs. Greek practice.** The saltwater *piscina* attached to a coastal villa was a late-Republican Italian aristocratic fashion: Varro (RR 3.17.2) complains that such ponds "are built at great cost, in the second place they are stocked at great cost, and in the third place they are kept up at great cost"; Columella (8.16–17) gives engineering details; Cicero mocked Lucullus and Hortensius as *piscinarii*. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/encyclopaedia_romana/wine/piscinae.html, MEDIUM] There is no comparable Classical Greek tradition of aristocratic fish-keeping; the rock-cut Roman tanks on Crete (§1.1) are the clearest sign of the Italian fashion arriving in the Aegean under the Empire (MEDIUM synthesis). The Byzantine *Geoponica* (20.1) later describes practical inland fishponds stocked with river or transplanted sea fish and fed "very small fish, and the gills and intestines of fish, and tender figs cut small, and soft cheese" – husbandry, not luxury. [https://archive.org/details/b22041072_0002, HIGH]

### 1.5 Athenaeus of Naucratis (c. 200) and the culture of fish-eating

Athenaeus' *Deipnosophistae* ("Sophists at Dinner," 15 books, c. AD 200) is the great compendium; Book 7 is essentially an alphabetical fish-lexicon compiled from lost Classical comedy and science. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deipnosophistae, MEDIUM] The key passage on why Greeks say *opson* for fish (Yonge translation, 7.276): "Although all the different dishes which we eat, besides the regular meal, are properly called by one generic name, ὄψον, still it is very deservedly that on account of its delicious taste fish has prevailed over everything else, and has appropriated the name to itself; because men are so exceedingly enamoured of this kind of food" – hence *opsophagoi*, "fish-eaters." [https://www.attalus.org/old/athenaeus7.html, HIGH] Athenaeus (7.304) also records that tuna was an expensive delicacy in the Roman period. [Çoruh Kurt 2025, DOI 10.33469/oannes.1611585, MEDIUM]

For salt fish specifically, John Wilkins ("Fish as a Source of Food in Antiquity") shows that Athenaeus and Galen are the richest sources on *tarichos*, e.g. Diphilus of Siphnos (quoted by Athenaeus): "salt-fish, whether from sea or lake or river, has [little nourishment]…", and Galen's fifteen chapters on fish including salted tuna from Spain. [Wilkins chapter PDF, MEDIUM]

## 2. The Byzantine Aegean (330–1453)

### 2.1 Constantinople, a city that lived on fish

- The Strabo/Pliny bonito story remained literally visible: Tournefort in 1701 wrote, "The Port of Constantinople abounds with Tunny-fish, call'd Pelamides by the Antients: we see them frequently represented on the Medals of Byzantium, with the Heads of the Emperors Caligula, Claudius, Caracalla, Geta … Pliny says, that under the water towards Chalcedon, there were white Rocks that scared the Tunnies, and forced 'em into the Port of Byzantium: Dolphins too sometimes appear there in such numbers, the Port swarms with 'em." (Letter XII) [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH]
- Tournefort also reports that "Procopius of Cesarea, who lived in the sixth Century, informs us, that the Fishermen took notice that their Nets, instead of sinking perpendicularly to the bottom of the Canal, were dragg'd from the North towards the South, when they came to a certain Depth" – the Bosporus undercurrent as known to Byzantine fishermen – "for the Bosphorus has in all times been very famous for fishing." (vol. 2) [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant02tour, HIGH for Tournefort; the Procopius reference itself MEDIUM]
- A 2025 Turkish survey (Z. B. Kalaycı, "Bizans'tan Osmanlı'ya İstanbul'da Balık Tüketiminin Kurumsal ve Sosyo-Kültürel Geçmişine Genel Bir Bakış," *Avrasya Uluslararası Araştırmalar Dergisi*, 2025) summarizes the Byzantine picture: fish weirs (*dalyan*) were already set in the waters around the city in East Roman times (citing Somçağ 1994); the Fourth-Crusade chronicler Gunther of Pairis is cited for a Constantinopolitan fishing fleet of about 1,600 boats obliged to land the daily catch inside the walls (citing Mango 2011); the fishmongers' guild had to buy fresh from the boats and sell only at the appointed quays, on pain of expulsion, flogging and exile. [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/5213859, MEDIUM (secondary; the 1,600-boat figure should be checked against Gunther before use)]
- Fish names in Byzantine literature: in the 12th-century satire *Timarion*, a newcomer to Hades is asked how many "mackerel, bonitos, tunnies, picarels [and] whitebait" he gets; the *Prodromic Poems* (Ptochoprodromos, 12th c.) give a recipe for *monokythron* ("one-pot"): "cabbage, five kinds of fish, fourteen eggs, three kinds of cheese, olive oil, pepper, garlic and sweet wine." (Charles Perry, "The Taste of Byzantium," *Cornucopia*) [https://www.cornucopia.net/magazine/articles/the-taste-of-byzantium/, MEDIUM] Perry adds that "Turkish is full of Greek-derived names for fishes" – the Byzantine vocabulary (palamut/palamida, uskumru/skoumbri, lüfer/loufari, etc.) passed into Ottoman usage. [same, MEDIUM] The standard modern study is Andrew Dalby, *Flavours of Byzantium* (Prospect, 2003; reissued as *Tastes of Byzantium*). [https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.02.34/, MEDIUM]

### 2.2 The *Book of the Eparch* (c. 895–912), chapter XVII: the fishmongers (ἰχθυοπράται)

Full text of the chapter, in E. H. Freshfield's translation (*Roman Law in the Later Roman Empire: Byzantine Guilds, Professional and Commercial. Ordinances of Leo VI c. 895 from the Book of the Eparch*, Cambridge 1938, pp. 40–41): [https://brittlebooks.library.illinois.edu/brittlebooks_closed/Books2009-03/0001romlaw/0001romlaw.pdf, HIGH]

> § 1. Where fishmongers congregate in the chief markets to sell fish in town, every such market shall have a master who shall ascertain the price paid for the fish at sea and the price paid for it ashore. These masters shall be entitled to a perquisite of one miliaresion per gold nomisma.
>
> § 2. Fishmongers are forbidden to salt fish and then sell it to aliens for export. They are, however, permitted to sell 'remainders' of fish on the market which might decompose.
>
> § 3. Fishmongers shall make their purchases on the sea beach or the piers from fishing smacks at anchor. They shall not go directly to the fishing grounds to buy on the high seas, but they shall wait till the fishing smacks make fast ashore. Indeed, the sale of fish shall not be made too much in retail. Their remuneration shall be two folleis per gold nomisma and the master of the guild shall receive four folleis.
>
> § 4. Let the masters of the guild of fishmongers go every day at daybreak to inform the eparch of the number of white fish caught during the night so that sales may be effected in town at the prices fixed by the eparch. Any contravener shall be flogged, shaved and expelled from the guild.

Freshfield's notes: "white fish" (τῶν λευκῶν ἰχθύων) he takes to mean "high-grade fish such as sturgeon, tunny or the like"; a *parathalassites* supervised the piers in the harbour of Theodosius II on the Marmara shore. [same, HIGH] The text was edited by Jules Nicole (1893); the 1970 Variorum reprint gathers Nicole, Freshfield, and a facsimile. [https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/byzantine-and-modern-greek-studies/article/abs/shape-of-the-market-mapping-the-book-of-the-eparch/15BCD7FE5E6C410323B432F0C2654B48, MEDIUM]

### 2.3 The *Geoponica*, Book 20 (10th c.)

A twenty-book agricultural compilation made in Constantinople for Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, drawing on Vindonius Anatolius (4th c.) and Cassianus Bassus (6th/7th c.); the only surviving Byzantine agricultural work. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoponica, MEDIUM] Book 20's own "hypothesis": it treats "the propagating of fish, and the bringing of them from different places into one spot, and concerning the taking of them, and the composition of all kinds of baits that are adapted to the taking of different river and sea fish." Its 46 chapters include: fishponds (1); "To bring fish to one place" (2); river fish (3–5); "Concerning weels" (28–29); baits for sea-mullets (15–17, 30), scorpii (31), phagri (32), garfish (33), "For tunnies only" (34), smarides (35), rays (36–37), salpa (38), trachuri/horse-mackerel (40), "For polypodes and sepiae" (22, 42–43), lobsters (44), melanuri (45), and the garum recipe (46). [Owen 1805 vol. 2, https://archive.org/details/b22041072_0002, HIGH]

Sample bait ("To bring fish to one place," 20.2): "Having separately pounded, and then mixed with fine sand, and having laid them in the place an hour or two before, pulegium, thymbra, origanum, sampsuchum, three drams of each; the bark of libanotus, myrrh, sinopis, eight drams of each; half a mina of meal of parched barley reduced to solution in well-flavoured wine; twenty-four drams of roasted hogs liver, an equal quantity of goat-suet, and as much garlic; set your nets. But some throw in the herb delphinium, pounded and sifted, and allure the fish, that they may take them with their hands." And for river fish (20.3): "Pound mutton suet, parched sesamum, garlic with well-flavoured wine, thyme, dried sampsuchum … make them up with bread, and throw them in." [same, HIGH] Chapters carry the names of the excerpted authorities (e.g., Florentinus, Didymus, Democritus, Tarentinus) in Owen's headings. [same, MEDIUM] H. J. Rose, "The Folklore of the Geoponica" (1933), is the classic study of its magical-folkloric content. [https://www.attalus.org/armenian/Folklore_Rose_1933_Geoponica.pdf, MEDIUM]

### 2.4 Fasting and the demand for fish and seafood

The **rule that shellfish and cephalopods are permitted even on strict fast days** is old and is directly attested by early-modern travellers:

- Thévenot, 1655: "The Greeks have four Lents … During these three last Lents, they may eat Fish and Oyl; but in the first Lent, they eat neither Oyl nor Fish, nor any thing that hath blood, but only Herbs and Shell-fish, and that which they call Ceppiæ, and we Cuttle-fish, whose blood is as black as Ink; and certainly what Busbequius says, That the Greeks never eat Oysters, is not true, for they hardly eat any thing else during Lent, and at all times they are great Eaters of Fish." (*Travels into the Levant*, 1687 English ed., Part I) [https://archive.org/details/travelsofmonsieu00thev, HIGH]
- Tournefort, 1700 (Letter II, on the Greek Church, 1718 ed. pp. 85–86): "in the first Week of this Lent, it is lawful to eat Cheese, Milk, Fish, and Eggs; all which they are forbidden during the following Weeks: they feed wholly upon Shell-fish, and such other as they believe to be without Blood, as are the Polypus and the Cuttle-fish; they also eat the Eggs of certain Fish salted, and especially those of the Mullet and Sturgeon: the first are prepared upon the Coasts of Ephesus and Miletus, and the others on those of the Black Sea [marginal gloss: Botargo, or Poutargue] … They eat Fish on Palm-Sunday, and the 25th of March, the Day of the Annunciation, provided that Day does not fall in the Holy Week." He then describes the Christmas Lent (forty days, fish allowed except Wednesdays and Fridays), the Apostles' Lent (fish allowed), and the Lent of the Virgin (1–15 August), in which "the use of Fish is forbidden … unless on the sixth of the same Month, which is the Day of our Saviour's Transfiguration; all the other days they are confined to Shell-fish and Pulse." Also: the monks "live upon nothing but some sorts of Fish, Pulse, Olives, dry'd Figs." [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH]
- The same rules today (Greek usage): "Fruits, vegetables, grains and shellfish are permitted throughout the year"; fish on Palm Sunday and the Annunciation (St George Greek Orthodox Cathedral, Manchester NH). [https://www.stgeorgenh.org/orthodox-resources/sacraments/fasting-guidelines/, MEDIUM] "All Wednesdays and Fridays … are kept as days of fasting, with no meat, eggs, dairy products, fish, wine or oil"; in Great Lent "fish is permitted as well as wine and oil" only on the Annunciation and Palm Sunday (All Saints, Toronto). [https://www.allsaintstoronto.ca/fasting-rules, MEDIUM] Wikipedia's *Great Lent* article adds that caviar is traditionally allowed on Lazarus Saturday. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lent, LOW]
- The widely quoted figure of "about 180 fast days a year" is arithmetically plausible (four Lents plus every Wednesday and Friday) but I found no authoritative source stating it; treat as LOW.
- A commercial site claims the bloodless-seafood rule was codified in a "Novel 58 of Leo VI." I could not verify this and the claim looks garbled; LOW/unverified. [https://nutri.it.com/is-shrimp-allowed-during-orthodox-lent-understanding-the-fasting-rules, LOW]

### 2.5 Byzantine fishing law

- **Leo VI's fishing Novels are the one part of his legislation that stayed in force.** Zachary Chitwood, *Byzantine Legal Culture and the Roman Legal Tradition, 867–1056* (Cambridge, 2017), ch. 1, quotes an anonymous legal history of c. 1080: "Lord Leon also composed 120 novels, but they are not all in use. Valid however is that which concerns [the distances between] fishing nets and pieces of land" – identifying Novels 102–104 (fishing nets/stations) and 71 (land distances) as those actually applied. [https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/byzantine-legal-culture-and-the-roman-legal-tradition-8671056/cleansing-of-the-ancient-laws-under-basil-i-and-leo-vi/0F03BA8BAF8AFBD2973BBE743B7DB459, HIGH]
- The Greek term ἐποχαί (*epochai*, "fishing stations/fixed-net sites") for these regulated stations, and the detailed rules on minimum distances between them along the Bosporus and Marmara shores, are discussed in Gilbert Dagron, "Poissons, pêcheurs et poissonniers de Constantinople," in C. Mango & G. Dagron (eds), *Constantinople and its Hinterland* (Aldershot 1995), pp. 57–73 – the standard study. I could not read Dagron, so the specific content of Novels 102–104 (which of them concern epochai, what distances) is **MEDIUM by reputation, not verified here**. [https://www.routledge.com/Constantinople-and-its-Hinterland-Papers-from-the-Twenty-Seventh-Spring-Symposium-of-Byzantine-Studies-Oxford-April-1993/Mango-Dagron/p/book/9780860784876, MEDIUM]
- **Roman law on the shore** (inherited via the *Basilika*, Leo VI's Greek recasting of Justinian's *Corpus*, c. 892): the seashore was common, but the *Digest* (41.1.14) allowed that a mole built into the sea became the builder's – the legal basis of private fish-tank ownership. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/encyclopaedia_romana/wine/piscinae.html, MEDIUM; Basilika date: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilika, MEDIUM]

### 2.6 Salt roe: *ootarichon*, botargo, caviar

- English *botargo* / Italian *bottarga* derive from Arabic *buṭārikh* (via Occitan *boutargo* for the English form), and the Arabic from Byzantine Greek ᾠοτάριχον (*ōotárichon*, "egg" + "pickled/salted"). The Greek word is first attested in the 11th-century Byzantine physician Simeon Seth, who advised avoiding it "totally"; the Italian form appears c. 1500, and Platina (c. 1474) has the Latinized *ova tarycha*. Modern Greek *avgotaracho*; Messolonghi's is a PDO product. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottarga and https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/botargo, MEDIUM]
- Kalaycı (citing Dalby 2014: 74): salted grey-mullet roe (*otarikhon*) kept its Classical prestige on Byzantine tables; by the 12th century Black Sea caviar (*kabiari*) was prized by the Byzantine elite. [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/5213859, MEDIUM]
- Tournefort's 1700 note (above) that mullet botargo was made "upon the Coasts of Ephesus and Miletus" (i.e., the lagoons of the Kaystros and Maeander mouths) and sturgeon roe on the Black Sea is the best early-modern statement of where Aegean botargo came from. [HIGH]

### 2.7 Liutprand of Cremona's complaint (968)

Liutprand, bishop of Cremona, went to Constantinople on 4 June 968 as Otto I's envoy to Nicephorus II Phocas; his *Relatio de legatione Constantinopolitana* is famously venomous. [https://www.medievalists.net/2020/04/liutprand-embassy-constantinople/, MEDIUM] In E. F. Henderson's translation (*Select Historical Documents of the Middle Ages*, 1910): the imperial dinner was a "disgusting and foul meal, which was washed down with oil after the manner of drunkards, and moistened also with a certain and other exceedingly bad fish liquor"; later the emperor sent him "a fat goat, of which he himself had partaken, deliciously stuffed with garlic, onions and leeks; steeped in fish sauce"; and another dish "smelt strongly of garlic and onions and was filthy with oil and fish-juice." [https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/liudprand1.asp, HIGH] The Latin word is *garum*; F. A. Wright's 1930 translation is the other standard English version. [https://archive.org/details/embassytoconstan0000liud, MEDIUM] The point for fishing history: garum/*garos* was still in everyday elite use in 10th-century Constantinople, three centuries after it had faded in the Latin West.

### 2.8 Fishing gear from Byzantine shipwrecks

- **Yassı Ada (off Bodrum), sunk c. 625/626** under Heraclius, excavated by George Bass 1961–64 and published as *Yassi Ada: A Seventh-Century Byzantine Shipwreck* (Texas A&M, 1982): a c. 21 m merchantman with c. 900 amphoras; the captain's steelyard is inscribed for "Georgios, elder [presbyteros] and sea-captain." [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yassi_Ada, MEDIUM] Among the galley finds were fishing nets, net weights and a fishing harpoon – the crew fished en route. [https://bodrumguides.com/2023/07/25/yassiada-wreck-scale/, MEDIUM (popular summary of Bass's report)]
- **Serçe Limanı ("Glass Wreck"), c. 1025**: the published inventory of personal possessions includes fishing gear alongside weapons, tools, cookware and scales. [https://muse.jhu.edu/book/2791, LOW–MEDIUM (from catalogue summary)]

## 3. Venetians, Franks and corsairs (1204–1715)

### 3.1 The Latin states

- **Duchy of the Archipelago (Naxos).** Founded 1207 by the Venetian Marco Sanudo with eight galleys borrowed from the Arsenal; Crispo family seized it in 1383; tributary to the Ottomans from 1537; last Latin duke Giacomo IV Crispo deposed by Selim II in 1566 and replaced by the Portuguese-Jewish financier Joseph Nasi (1566–79); annexed outright by Murad III in 1579. It comprised most of the Cyclades except Mykonos and Tinos (held by the Ghisi); Naxian emery and marble were exported to Venice. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_the_Archipelago, MEDIUM; William Miller, *The Latins in the Levant* (1908) is the classic narrative, MEDIUM]
- **Tinos and Mykonos**: ruled by the Ghisi 1207–1390, then bequeathed to Venice; Tinos stayed Venetian until captured by the Ottomans in 1715 – the last Venetian island in the Aegean proper. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinos, MEDIUM]
- **Crete (Kingdom of Candia)**: bought from Boniface of Montferrat in 1204 for 1,000 marks, secured by 1212 after fighting off the Genoese; exports of wheat, malmsey wine, some timber and cheese; 27 documented uprisings; lost 1645–69 except the forts of Souda, Gramvousa and Spinalonga, which fell in 1715. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Candia, MEDIUM] Venetian Crete also produced salt under the Republic's state monopoly. [https://www.venicethefuture.com/schede/uk/174-aliusid=174.htm, LOW]
- **Negroponte (Euboea)**: Venetian from the 13th c.; besieged by Mehmed II 14 June–12 July 1470 and taken with a three-day sack; over 6,000 defenders killed. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Negroponte_(1470), MEDIUM]
- **Kythera (Cerigo)**: Venetian (Venier family, then the Republic) until Napoleon extinguished Venice in 1797. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kythira, MEDIUM]

### 3.3 The Cretan War (1645–1669) and the siege of Candia

Pretext: in 1644 the Knights of Malta seized an Ottoman convoy bound for Mecca with senior officials aboard; the Porte blamed Venice. Candia was besieged from May 1648 to its surrender on 5 September 1669 – roughly 21 years; Venetian records count c. 30,985 Venetian and 118,754 Ottoman dead at Candia; Venice spent some 4,253,000 ducats on its defence and kept Tinos, Kythera and the three Cretan island-forts. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_War_(1645%E2%80%931669), MEDIUM] Effect on Aegean islanders: Thévenot at Kea (Zia) in 1655 found the islanders paying tribute to both belligerents – "The Inhabitants pay yearly in Caradge or Tribute three thousand four hundred Piastres to the Turks, and two thousand six hundred to the Venetians, besides the extortions and robberies they meet with; so that the Inhabitants being thereby ruined and oppressed, many of them are forced to forsake their [homes]." [https://archive.org/details/travelsofmonsieu00thev, HIGH]

### 3.4 Corsairs in the 17th-century Archipelago

- The Maltese *corso* (1530–1798) and the Barbary corsairs of Algiers/Tunis/Tripoli were the two great predatory systems; French, Provençal and Livornese privateers joined during the Venetian-Ottoman wars. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_Corso, MEDIUM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_pirates, MEDIUM]
- Naoussa (Paros) was noted as a pirates' port as early as Buondelmonti's 1420 manuscript; Paros, Syros, Mykonos, Milos and Kimolos served as corsair bazaars and winter havens; in 1677 an Ottoman force landed at Naoussa with 25 galleys and arrested the inhabitants. Named corsairs: Temericourt, Crevelier ("Creviller"), Angelo Maria Vidali. [https://parola-paros-freepress.gr/en/piracy-in-the-aegean/, LOW–MEDIUM (local press, no citations)]
- **Wheler, 1676**: villagers in Attica had, through the French consul at Athens, made "an Agreement between them and Crevelly, the chief of the Pirates of the Archipelago, to give him yearly an hundred and fifty Measures of Corn, to let them live in quiet." (*A Journey into Greece*, 1682, Bk VI, p. 432) [https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-journey-into-greece-_wheler-sir-george_1682, HIGH]
- **Tournefort, 1700** (the corsair era had just ended after Louis XIV suppressed the French privateers):
  - Argentiera (Kimolos): "this Island is become wretchedly poor ever since the King put down the French Corsairs in the Levant. Argentiere used to be the place of their Rendevouz, where they spent in horrible Debaucheries the Booty they took from the Turks." (Letter IV) [HIGH]
  - Milos (p. 119): "The Miliotes are good Sailors; being much used to the Archipelago, they serve as Pilots to most Ships trading thither from abroad. When the French Corsairs were masters of the Sea in the Levant, this Island abounded with all manner of Accommodations: they still have in their mouths the Atchievements and heroick Actions of Messieurs Beneville, Temericourt, Chevalier d'Hoquincour, Hugh Crevelier, and others who used to bring in their Prizes thither, as the principal Fair of the Archipelago … The Turks are more insolent than ever in the Islands, since the disappearing of the French Corsairs … the Corsairs kept the Turks in awe." [HIGH]
  - Sifnos: "The Siphantines, to get rid of 'em [Ottoman-Jewish lead-mine prospectors] at once, gave a Sum of Mony to a Corsair of Provence, who was at Milo, and who cannonaded a second Galliot laden with Jews and Lead-Oar … The famousest Corsairs of the Archipelago had nothing odious but the Name of Corsair. They were Men of Quality and distinguish'd Valour." [HIGH]
  - Ios (Nio): the inhabitants "are all Thieves by Profession, and therefore the Turks call it Little Malta; 'tis a Harbouring-place for most of the Corsairs of the Mediterranean." (Letter VI) [HIGH]
  - Sikinos (p. 199): "The French Corsairs that marry there, are exempt from the Capitation … There can't be a greater Punishment than for an old Fisherman to marry in Greece; their Wives have neither Virtue nor Mony." [HIGH]
  - Delos and Mykonos: the corsairs called the Lesser Delos "the School"; the Myconiots pasture cattle on Great Delos "but in regard they are often visited by the Corsairs, who come thither for Quarters of Refreshment, the Myconiots transport their Flocks back into their own Island"; the monks of Amorgos on the islet of Herakleia "are every moment alarm'd by the Corsairs, who often land there to catch a few Goats." (Letters VI–VII) [HIGH]
  All Tournefort quotations: [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH]
- The Cretan Maritime Museum's exhibition note frames the 17th-century Venetian-Ottoman wars (1645–69, 1684–99) as turning the Cyclades into a no-man's-land where "Paros was a corsairs' bazaar, along with Syros and Mykonos." [http://mar-mus-crete.gr/en/2017/11/02/, LOW (page not retrievable at time of writing)]
- Modern synthesis: *Piracy in the Eastern Mediterranean: Maritime Marauders in the Greek and Ottoman Aegean* (I.B. Tauris/Bloomsbury). [https://dokumen.pub/piracy-in-the-eastern-mediterranean-maritime-marauders-in-the-greek-and-ottoman-aegean-9780755606696-9780755643608-9780755606726-9780755606719.html, MEDIUM]

## 4. The Ottoman Aegean (c. 1450–1820)

### 4.1 Ottoman fishing taxes: *dalyan*, *iltizam*, *balıkhane*

From Kalaycı 2025 (Ottoman archival study, Istanbul-focused) [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/5213859, all MEDIUM unless noted]:

- The *Balık Emini* ("Fish Warden"), chosen from the *haseki*s of the Bostancı Corps, was both tax-farmer (*mültezim*) of the fish weirs (*dalyan*) around Istanbul and responsible for delivering the catch to the *balıkhane* (fish house). The original balıkhane stood near Unkapanı; administration passed to the Bostancı Ocağı in 1735 and the fish house moved to Galata toward the end of the 18th century, remaining Istanbul's only balıkhane for about two centuries (citing Doğan 2011; Ertuğ 2015; Uluskan 2015).
- Dalyans were *mukataa* units farmed out annually (*iltizam*) under the Istanbul Customs; named weir sites include Tuzla, Darıca, Pendik, Kartal, Beykoz, Büyükdere, Sarıyer, Kavak, İncirli, İstinye, the two Hisars, İstavroz, Kuruçeşme, Beşiktaş and Kuzguncuk (archival ref. BOA MAD.d 3233).
- Evliya Çelebi (17th c.) names 75 fish species and counts some 300 dalyans around Istanbul with about 700 weir-fishermen; the largest, at Beykoz, specialized in swordfish; twenty dalyans were state (*miri*) property. He lists gear-specific guilds: *dalyancı* (weir-men) taking swordfish, mackerel, bonito, bluefish, mullet, horse-mackerel and anchovy; *ığrıpçı* (beach-seiners dragging nets along the shore); *karityacı* (long-pole fishers in the Golden Horn); *ağcı* (rock-cast nets); *saçmacı* (cast-net fishers working at night close inshore); *düzenci* (harbour and cove fishers for gobies, picarel and horse-mackerel); *zıpkıncı* (harpooners at the passes); and pot/basket trappers. About 1,000 hook-and-line fishermen with 600 shops and 300 net-fishermen with 70 shops are cited (Mantran 1990).
- Individual permits: in 1705 a non-Muslim named Zefiri was licensed to set up a dalyan at Kilyos on the Black Sea against a one-fifth tax (BOA AE.SAMD.III 74/7445); in 1712 the Ağa of Rumelihisarı bought a fishing licence for a fee to the treasury and the fish wardens (BOA C.ML 196/8106). [HIGH – archival citations given in the article]
- Prices were fixed by *narh*; the article argues for explicit institutional continuity from the Byzantine *ichthyopratai*/Eparch to the Ottoman balıkçı esnafı and Balık Emini.

For the islands themselves the best figures are travellers':

- **Naxos, 1700** (Tournefort, Letter V): "They still catch abundance of Mullets and Eels in this Port [the 'Port of the Fishpond,' now 'Port of the Saltpits'], by means of certain Hurdles of Reeds fasten'd together: these Hurdles fold like our Skreens, and are so order'd, that the Fish which get into them at holes left on purpose, cannot disengage themselves … The Fishery of Naxia, the Customs, and the Saltpits of the Town, are farm'd but at 800 Crowns." [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH] This is a fishery and salt-works let as a single tax-farm – the island-level equivalent of the Istanbul dalyan *iltizam*.
- **Ikaria, 1655** (Thévenot): the islanders "pay the Grand Signior their Tribute in Sponges" (see §4.2). [HIGH]
- **Symi, 1786**: an Ottoman order of 11 April 1786 (11 Cemaziyelahir 1200) required Symi, held as an *ocaklık* for the palace, to deliver 15,000 sponges – 3,000 fine (*has*) and 12,000 coarse (BOA C.SM 39/1978); the later sponge tax was systematized as a one-fifth (*humus*) share. (A. Baykara Taşkaya, "An Island Skilled in Sponge Hunting 'Symi' (1786–1909)," *Journal of Universal History Studies* 5/1 (2022) 51–78) [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/2352361, HIGH]

### 4.2 Sponge diving

- **Antiquity**: Oppian's sponge-cutter (§1.2) already carries a lead weight, a cutting bill and oil in his mouth; sponges are in Homer and Plato as bath objects. [Oppian, HIGH; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_diving, MEDIUM]
- **1655, Ikaria** – the earliest early-modern eyewitness I found: the Icarians "are much addicted to Swimming, and fishing up Sponges from the bottom of the Sea, or the Goods of Ships that have been cast away; and Batchelours are not married in this Island, unless they can dive at least eight fathom deep into the water … the young Men strip themselves stark naked before all the People … and he that stays longest under, obtains the Maid in Marriage. These are a sort of People that seem to be Fish, rather than Men. They pay the Grand Signior their Tribute in Sponges, and from them all Turkie is furnished." (Thévenot, Part I, ch. LXX "Samos and Nicaria") [https://archive.org/details/travelsofmonsieu00thev, HIGH]
- **17th century, Symi** – Evliya Çelebi describes Symiots who "dived 100 fathoms" (hyperbole) with a sponge-cutting awl at the waist and "did not give a girl to those who do not dive 100 fathoms" – the same bride-test motif as on Ikaria. [Baykara Taşkaya 2022 citing Evliya, HIGH for the citation]
- **The stone (*skandalopetra*)**: "Diving naked and unarmed … The only instrument used by such divers was a stone that is 30 to 40 centimeters long and weighed around 15 kilograms. One of the ends of this stone was pierced and a rope was passed through … The diver, holding the head of the stone with both hands, holds his breath and dives with his head heading down. Due to its shape and weight, the stone acted as a rudder." [Baykara Taşkaya 2022, HIGH] Divers reached c. 30 m for 3–5 minutes. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_diving, MEDIUM]
- **Ottoman privileges**: Kalymnos and neighbouring "barren islands" won privileges from the sultans after 1522 and defended them; a local-history site adds sponge-fishing rights "throughout all the seas of the Ottoman Empire" (LOW). Symi alone paid its tribute in sponges (12,000 coarse + 3,000 fine) per the municipality's history page (LOW as a page; the 1786 archival order above corroborates the figures, so MEDIUM overall). [https://www.in2greece.com/english/places/summer/islands/kalymnos-history.html, LOW; https://www.symi.gr/community/the-history-of-sponge-diving/?lang=en, LOW]
- **Later (outside the period, for orientation)**: the standard diving suit (*skafandro*) arrived c. 1865; in 1882 the sultan, petitioned by island women, banned it for a few years. [https://www.divingheritage.com/greecekern2.htm, LOW–MEDIUM] The authoritative modern monograph is Evdokia Olympitou, *Aegean Sponge Fishing and the Island of Kalymnos (19th–20th Centuries)* (Brill, 2024). [https://books.google.de/books/about/Aegean_Sponge_Fishing_and_the_Island_of.html?id=8PokEQAAQBAJ, MEDIUM]
- The claim that skandalopetra diving was "first" done by the Symiot Michael Karanikis in 1840 (municipality page) is contradicted by the 1655 and 17th-century evidence above and should be dropped. [LOW]

### 4.3 Hydra, Spetses, Psara: from Ottoman sailors to merchant powers

- Under the Ottomans "an important percentage of sailors were Greeks, doing their obligatory 'military service' aboard Ottoman vessels"; at the outbreak of the 1821 revolt the Porte arrested and executed Greek sailors then serving in its navy. The Ottoman navy relied on subject maritime peoples because "the Turks were not themselves a naval people." (Economou, Kyriazis & Prassa, *The Greek merchant fleet as a national navy during the war of independence 1800–1830*, MPRA 2016) [https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/76414/1/MPRA_paper_76414.pdf, HIGH]
- The take-off came with the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774): Christian Ottoman subjects could trade under the Russian flag "as a flag of opportunity" (citing Kremmydas 1985; Harlaftis 2001); interrupted 1787–92, then Greeks also flew Maltese, Austrian, British and Sardinian colours, and profited hugely running the British blockade of French ports during the Napoleonic wars. [same, HIGH]
- Hydra: "In the late 18th century and early 19th century, Hydra develops a great naval power with a commercial fleet of 150 ships"; Hydriots "served in the Turkish fleet," and the sultan's *nazir* Georgios Voulgaris administered the island and suppressed piracy. [https://www.hydra.gr/mobile/en_proepanastatikoi.html, MEDIUM (municipal site)]
- Spetses: seized by the Ottomans in 1715 but "very soon … given considerable privileges which eventually amounted to autonomy"; Pouqueville (*Voyage de la Grèce*, 1826–27, VI, 294–297) says that c. 1813 Spetses had 60 ships of 19,500 tons, 2,700 seamen and 900 guns. [https://jupiter.chem.uoa.gr/qscp8/spetsai.html, MEDIUM]
- Psara had the third-largest fleet after Hydra and Spetses, c. 45 ships by the early 19th century. [search snippet, LOW] Pococke's Psara in the 1740s was still humble: "They have no trade but the export of their wine, and the import of corn … as it is an open bay, they draw up their little barks and boats to the land … some countrymen who were eating bread and fish, called to me to take part with them." (*Description of the East* II.2, ch. III "Of the island of Ipsara") [https://archive.org/details/b30456307_0005, HIGH]
- Pococke also notes the corsair pressure on shipbuilding at Mytilene (Lesbos): "They have a great trade in this city in building large ships and boats with the wood of pine … they bring the timber from the continent, there being no place there secure from the Corsairs for the building of them." (ch. IV) [same, HIGH]

### 4.4 Island economies, 1600s–1700s (Tournefort unless stated)

- **Santorini**: "abundance of Cotton, and Wine in profusion"; the men are "abroad selling their Wines" (Letter VI). Its wine trade to Russia and the Santorinian sailing fleet are described in modern tourism sources (LOW). [Tournefort HIGH; https://www.greekgastronomyguide.gr/en/santorini/topos-istoria/, LOW] The local "vaporia" (steamships) belong to the 19th century; I found no pre-1820 source for the term.
- **Tinos** (still Venetian in 1700): "The Riches of Tinos consists at present in its Silk"; silk shipped to Venice paid no duties (Letter VIII). **Andros**: "The principal Riches of Andros consist in Silk." **Kea (Zia) and Kythnos (Thermia)**: silk and *velani* (valonia oak acorns for tanning). **Ios**: wheat; islanders "all Thieves by Profession." **Sikinos**: corn, sold clandestinely to Provençal ships in the 1700 dearth. **Milos**: pilots and corsair market (above). **Mykonos**: pastoralists on Delos, capitation 4,000 crowns in 1700 for one island in that letter (the island is not certain from the OCR – MEDIUM). **Amorgos**: no wood except cedar and mastic (see §7 for its use in torch-fishing). [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH except where noted]
- **Kea, 1655** (Thévenot, ch. XII): "its harbour is full of Fish, which we often made tryal of with our Nets." **A neighbouring island** (ch. XIII, the OCR context suggests Andros): the peasants "make very white wicker Baskets, which are used all over the Archipelago … Their Sea affords no Fish, and is, as to that, worse than the Sea of Gezoz [Zia?]." [https://archive.org/details/travelsofmonsieu00thev, HIGH for the text; the identification of the second island MEDIUM]
- **Samos, c. 1739** (Pococke, ch. VII): "The town of Vathi lives by fishing, and by an export of wine, which is very good, especially a white muscadine sort." [https://archive.org/details/b30456307_0005, HIGH]
- **Koutali (Marmara), c. 1740** (Pococke): "was formerly all covered with vineyards; but now the inhabitants apply more to the fishing trade: This island pays also four or five hundred dollars a year" – the whole island being rented by its Christian elders (*proto-gerai*), "as are most of the small islands, both in the Propontis and Archipelago." [same, HIGH]
- **Constantinople provisioning** (Tournefort, vol. 2): "The best Fish in the World is taken in the Levant. Beside the sorts we know, the Black Sea furnishes a number of others which are unknown to us." And of a Black-Sea port he identifies by its coins as a Roman colony: "Except the Cordage they send to Constantinople, they deal in nothing but Salt-fish and Train-oil. Their chief Salt-fish are Mackrels, and Pilchers or young Tunnies: the Oil is drawn from Dolphins and Sea-Calves." [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant02tour, HIGH; identity of the port not confirmed from the OCR, LOW]

## 5. 1683 in the Aegean (context for a map published that year)

- Kara Mustafa's siege of Vienna ran 14 July–12 September 1683 and ended in the Ottoman rout by Sobieski's relief army; the Holy League (Empire, Poland, Venice) followed in 1684. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna, MEDIUM]
- Venice, seeing the Porte engaged on the Danube, declared war on 25 April 1684 – "for the first and only time in the Ottoman–Venetian Wars" it was Venice that declared. Operations ranged from Dalmatia to the Aegean; Venetian forces occupied Naxos in June 1689, took Chios in September 1694 and evacuated it in February 1695 after the double battle of the Oinousses (9 and 19 February 1695) against Mezzomorto Hüseyin; the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) gave Venice the Morea but nothing in the Aegean. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morean_War, MEDIUM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Oinousses_Islands, MEDIUM]
- Two years before the map: on 23 July 1681 Duquesne's French squadron, chasing Tripolitan corsairs into Chios harbour, bombarded the town with some 4,000 rounds; Louis XIV later sent compensatory gifts to Mehmed IV. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardment_of_Chios_(1681), MEDIUM]
- So in 1683 the Cyclades were fourteen years past the fall of Candia, Tinos and Kythera still Venetian, the French privateers at the height of their Milos–Kimolos "fair," and the Morean War about to begin – the exact world Tournefort describes in retrospect in 1700 (§3.4). [synthesis, MEDIUM]

## 6. Fish in Greek Orthodox culture

- **Fasting** – see §2.4 for the Lenten rules (fish on the Annunciation, 25 March, and Palm Sunday; shellfish and cephalopods "without blood" always allowed; fish on the Transfiguration, 6 August, within the Dormition fast). [Tournefort 1718 pp. 85–86, HIGH; Thévenot 1687, HIGH]
- **ΙΧΘΥΣ** – acronym of Ἰησοῦς Χριστός Θεοῦ Υἱός Σωτήρ; in use by the late 2nd century; Tertullian: "we, little fishes, after the image of our Ichthys, Jesus Christ, are born in the water"; Clement of Alexandria recommends the fish as a seal device; early-3rd-century stele ΙΧΘΥΣ ΖΩΝΤΩΝ in the National Roman Museum; catacombs of Priscilla and San Sebastiano. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichthys, MEDIUM]
- **St Peter and St Andrew** were Galilean fishermen called from their nets (Mark 1:16–18; Matthew 4:18–20); Andrew is by tradition the founder-apostle of Byzantium's church. [Gospel text, HIGH; the Andrew–Byzantium tradition, MEDIUM]
- **St Nicholas of Myra** – "his primary role is as the protector of sailors and seamen"; Greek ships carry his icon on the bridge; captains vow *tamata* in the form of silver ships; from his feast (6 December) boats (*karavakia*) are decorated until Epiphany. [https://www.stnicholascenter.org/around-the-world/customs/greece, MEDIUM] He is routinely also called patron of fishermen in Greek popular usage, but I found no authoritative source separating "fishermen" from "sailors"; treat the fishermen attribution as customary, LOW–MEDIUM.
- **Epiphany/Theophany (6 January)** – the Great Blessing of the Waters is performed twice, at the vigil and on the feast, the priest immersing a hand-cross three times; "in Greek practice, a cross is cast into the water, and volunteers attempt to retrieve it." [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Blessing_of_Waters and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphany_(holiday), MEDIUM] The rite as such is early Byzantine; I could not verify an origin date for the specific custom of youths diving for the cross (see "Things I could not verify").

## 7. Techniques and boats

- **The four modes** – hook, net, trap (weel), trident – are exactly Oppian's fourfold classification (Bk 3), unchanged in the Aegean until the 20th century. [§1.2, HIGH]
- ***Trata*** (beach seine and the boat that works it): Modern Greek τράτα is from Venetian *trata* < Italian *tratto* < Latin *tractus* "dragged" – a Venetian-era loan for a technique already in Oppian's list of "seines" and "draw-nets." [https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/τράτα, MEDIUM; Oppian Bk 3, HIGH] The *trata* dance of Megara mimics hauling the net. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trata_(dance), LOW–MEDIUM] Evliya's Istanbul *ığrıpçı* (from Greek γρῖπος, seine) are the same trade. [Kalaycı 2025, MEDIUM] The Compton survey describes the *trata* boat as "a narrow, double-ended fishing boat … usually rowed but sometimes carried a one- or two-mast lateen rig." [Nic Compton, "Traditional Boats of Greece," *WoodenBoat* 247, https://www.naftotopos.gr/images/n_Articles/16001/TradlGreekBoats247-02.pdf, MEDIUM]
- **Night fishing by fire (*pyrofani*)** – the evidence chain is unusually good: Oppian Bk 4 (torch and trident, 2nd c. AD) [HIGH]; Aelian NA 2.8 (Euboean brazier-lanterns on the prow, with dolphins, 3rd c.) [HIGH]; Tournefort at Amorgos, 1700: "The Greeks make use of this Cedar to go a fishing, or rather a spearing with: they break it into small pieces, which they lay over a Gridiron at the Stern of their Galley-boat, and burn it in the night-time, thereby to draw the Fish to 'em by means of the Light it casts, which while the Fish are following, they strike 'em with their Tridents or three-fork'd Javelins: this Wood is brought to Amorgos from Caloyero, Cheiro, Skinosa, and other adjoining Rocks." (Letter VI) [https://archive.org/details/voyageintolevant01tour, HIGH]; Evliya's *saçmacı* casting nets at night inshore. [MEDIUM]
- **Fixed traps and weirs** – Naxos reed-hurdle weirs for mullet and eel (Tournefort, above) [HIGH]; Bosporus *dalyan*s (Byzantine *epochai*; Ottoman iltizam) [§2.5, §4.1]; Geoponica ch. 28–29 "Concerning weels." [HIGH]
- **Tuna lookouts** – Oppian Bk 3 and Aelian 15.5 (pine-trunk towers, lead-weighted nets, six oarsmen a side) [HIGH]; Kyzikos watchtowers attested by Strabo and inscriptions [Çoruh Kurt 2025, HIGH].
- **Bait lore** – Geoponica Bk 20 (herb-and-liver bait balls; delphinium to stupefy; species-specific baits). [HIGH]
- **Boats**:
  - *Caïque/kaïki*: English via French *caïque* < Italian *caicco* < Ottoman Turkish *kayık*; Greek καΐκι directly from Turkish; the word is attested in 17th-century French. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca%C3%AFque, MEDIUM] To Greeks it "describes all small- or medium-sized traditional wooden boats." [Compton, MEDIUM]
  - *Trechandiri*: the classic double-ender with the raised stem-head (*koraki*). Per the historian Kostas Damianidis (as reported by Compton), "the first trehandiri was built on Hydra in 1658 by two islanders 'who were repatriated after capture by pirates,'" perhaps on the model of Adriatic *trabaccolo*; 1,061 trechandiria were built in 1843–58, 356 at Spetses, 251 at Hydra, 255 at Koroni. [Compton, MEDIUM; the 1658 story is a tradition – LOW as a dated fact]
  - *Perama*, *tserniki* (with a *sakoleva* sprit rig claimed to go back to the 2nd–3rd c. AD – LOW), *varkalas* (transom-sterned), *gatzao*, *symiaki skafi* (the sponge-fleet's tserniki type). [Compton, MEDIUM; Hellenic Maritime Museum, https://www.hmmuseum.gr/en-us/Collections/Permanent-Exhibition/Traditional-Shipbuilding-and-Ships, MEDIUM] Tournefort's "Galley-boat" at Amorgos and Thévenot's "small Barks" of Ikaria are the 17th–18th-century ancestors; no period source names a *trechandiri*.
  - Pococke's Mytilene yards built ships "with the wood of pine, which they use even to the keels … They use also iron nails in building; and instead of crooked timber, they saw the [frames]." [HIGH]
- **Gear in wrecks** – Yassı Ada (626): nets, weights, harpoon. [§2.8, MEDIUM]

## 8. Things I could not verify (or that need a better source before you repeat them)

1. **Any excavated Roman fish-salting installation (cetaria) in the Aegean islands or mainland Greece.** Lytle 2018 and Mylona 2018 discuss the *absence*; the Cretan "fish tanks" are holding tanks, not salting vats. Pliny names only Clazomenae in the region.
2. **The contents of Leo VI's Novels 102–104 (epochai distances).** Only Chitwood's report of the c. 1080 treatise ("that which concerns the distances between fishing nets") is verified; Dagron 1995 is the study to consult.
3. **The "1,600 fishing boats" of Constantinople** (Gunther of Pairis, via Kalaycı via Mango) – check the primary text.
4. **"About 180 fast days a year"** – plausible, not sourced.
5. **"Leo VI Novel 58" as the origin of the bloodless-seafood rule** – found only on a commercial site; probably garbled.
6. **Venetian fishing ordinances or fish-tithes for Crete** – nothing found.
7. **Prodromic-poem fish-name lists** – only the *monokythron* recipe and the *Timarion* list were verified (Perry); Dalby 2003, pp. 66–69, is where the full lists are discussed.
8. **Serçe Limanı fishing gear** – catalogue summary only.
9. **Michael Karanikis (Symi, 1840) as "first" skandalopetra diver** – contradicted by 1655/17th-century evidence; drop.
10. **Kalymnos sponge-fishing "rights throughout the Ottoman seas" after 1522** – local-history claim, no document.
11. **St Nicholas as patron of *fishermen* specifically** (vs. sailors) – customary, not documented here.
12. **The origin date of the Epiphany cross-diving custom** – rite is ancient; the diving contest's date is unverified.
13. **Santorini "vaporia" and the Russian Eucharistic-wine story** – tourism sources only.
14. **Sakoleva rig "dating back to the 2nd–3rd century AD"** – unsupported in the source that states it.
15. **The identity of two islands in the OCR'd travellers** (Thévenot's "sea affords no fish" island, probably Andros; Tournefort's "4,000 crowns" island in Letter VIII) – check against page images.
16. **Choiseul-Gouffier's text** was not examined for fishing content.
17. **The 1683 map's own provenance** is outside this dossier's scope.
