## 4. Why "the Archipelago"

- The word entered European languages from Italian *arcipelago*, attested in the 13th century as a proper name **for the Aegean Sea specifically**. Although its parts are Greek (ἀρχι- "chief" + πέλαγος "sea"), no such word exists in ancient or medieval Greek; it is probably an Italian alteration of Medieval Latin *Egeopelagus* ("Aegean sea"). Because the Aegean is so crowded with islands, the name generalised (c. 1600 in English) to any island-studded sea and then to island groups. HIGH — S65, S66.
- Politically, "the Archipelago" was a state: the **Duchy of the Archipelago** (also Duchy of Naxos), founded in 1207 by the Venetian Marco Sanudo after the Fourth Crusade, ruled by the Sanudo (1207–1383) and Crispo (1383–1566) dynasties from Naxos. It became an Ottoman tributary in 1537, its last Latin duke Giacomo IV Crispo was deposed in 1566, and Sultan Selim II gave the duchy to the Portuguese-Jewish financier Joseph Nasi (1566–79); on Nasi's death in 1579 it was annexed outright. HIGH — S67, S68.
- The Ottomans kept the name: the islands became the **Sanjak of Nakşa Berre** (Naxos–Paros) within the Kapudan Pasha's Eyalet of the Archipelago (by 1600), with beys recorded at Naxos, Milos and Santorini in 1629. HIGH — S69.
- So on this 1683 map "Archipel" is simultaneously the sea, the island group, and the memory of a Latin duchy that had disappeared a century earlier.

## 5. The Aegean in 1683: political context

| Point | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nearly every island on the map is Ottoman. Northern Aegean: Thasos 1456, Samothrace 1457, Imbros 1455/1479, Lemnos 1456/1479. Euboea (Negroponte) taken from Venice 12 July 1470. Northern Sporades (Skiathos, Skopelos, Skyros) taken by Barbarossa in 1538. Cyclades: tributary from 1537, formally annexed 1579; the last Latin lords (Gozzadini on Kythnos and Sifnos) ousted in 1617. Rhodes 1522. | HIGH | S19, S20, S22, S24, S28, S30, S32, S33, S44, S49, S67, S70 |
| **Crete (Candie)**: the Cretan War (1645–69) ended with the surrender of Candia on **5 September 1669** after a siege that had begun in May 1648. Venice kept only the offshore forts of Souda, Spinalonga and Gramvousa (lost 1715). Mallet notes Cretan refugees settling on Cerigo. | HIGH | S71, S59, S2 |
| **Venetian survivals in 1683**: Tinos (until 1715 — "the last Venetian possessions in the Aegean, the islands of Tinos and Aigina" fell to the Ottoman fleet in that war) and Kythera/Cerigo (until 1797). Mallet marks both with garrisons "qui appartient aux Venitiens". The Ionian Islands (off the map's west edge) were also Venetian. | HIGH | S36, S37, S72, S57, S2 |
| **Piracy**: Mallet repeatedly notes islands "presque desert à cause des frequentes insultes des Corsaires" (Mykonos, Anafi) and that Christian corsairs used Delos ("les Sdilles") as a base — the Aegean of 1683 was a raiding ground for both Barbary and Christian (Maltese) corsairs. | HIGH (Mallet's testimony) | S2 |
| **On the eve of war**: the failed Ottoman siege of Vienna (Sept. 1683), the Holy League of Linz (5 March 1684), and Venice's declaration of war on the Porte (25 April 1684) opened the **Morean War (1684–99)**: Morosini took Coron 1685, Nauplia 1686, Patras and Athens 1687; the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) confirmed Venice's Kingdom of the Morea, which the Ottomans retook in 1714–15. The map was printed in the last months of the pre-war order. | HIGH | S73, S60 |
| Later: Tinos, Aegina and the Cretan forts fell in 1715; the Treaty of Passarowitz (1718) left the Ottomans in possession of the Morea and Crete. | HIGH | S72, S59 |
