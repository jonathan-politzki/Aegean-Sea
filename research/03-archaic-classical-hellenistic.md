## 1. Homer: what the epics know about fishing

The heroes of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* do not eat fish at table, but the poet plainly knows fishing gear intimately: five similes describe rod, line, bronze hook, horn guard, lead sinker, and beach seine. All five line references below were checked against the Greek/Murray text on Perseus.

### 1.1 *Iliad* 16.404–410 — Patroclus hauls Thestor out of his chariot like an angler
Patroclus spears Thestor through the jaw and "laid hold of the spear and dragged him over the chariot-rim, as when a man sitting upon a jutting rock draggeth to land a sacred fish from out the sea, with line and gleaming hook of bronze; even so on the bright spear dragged he him agape from out the car" (Murray). The simile proper is 16.406–408. Note the "sacred fish" (ἱερὸν ἰχθύν) — a phrase already puzzled over in antiquity. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0134%3Abook%3D16%3Acard%3D394, HIGH]

### 1.2 *Iliad* 24.80–82 — Iris dives like a lead sinker on an ox-horn
Iris plunges into the sea "like a plummet of lead, the which, set upon the horn of an ox of the field, goeth down bearing death to the ravenous fishes" (Murray, 24.80–82). This is the earliest description of a weighted line; the horn tube is generally read as a guard over the line just above the hook, to stop fish biting through the line. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0134%3Abook%3D24%3Acard%3D77, HIGH for the text; MEDIUM for the "line-guard" interpretation, which is scholarly consensus rather than stated in the poem]

### 1.3 *Odyssey* 12.251–255 — Scylla takes six men like a rock-angler taking fish
"And as a fisher on a jutting rock, when he casts in his baits as a snare to the little fishes, with his long pole lets down into the sea the horn of an ox of the steading, and then as he catches a fish flings it writhing ashore, even so were they drawn writhing up towards the cliffs" (Murray, 12.251–255). Long rod (ῥάβδος), bait, ox-horn again. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D12%3Acard%3D234, HIGH]

### 1.4 *Odyssey* 4.368–369 — Menelaus's men fish from hunger on Pharos
Marooned twenty days off Egypt, Menelaus's comrades "were ever roaming about the island, fishing with bent hooks, for hunger pinched their bellies" (Murray, 4.368–369). This is the only place in Homer where anyone actually fishes for food, and it is a mark of desperation. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D4%3Acard%3D351, HIGH]

### 1.5 *Odyssey* 22.384–388 — the dead suitors like netted fish on a beach
Odysseus finds the suitors "fallen in the blood and dust—all the host of them, like fishes that fishermen have drawn forth in the meshes of their net from the grey sea upon the curving beach, and they all lie heaped upon the sand, longing for the waves of the sea, and the bright sun takes away their life" (Murray, 22.383–388). A shore seine (δίκτυον πολυωπόν, "many-meshed net") hauled up a beach. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D22%3Acard%3D378, HIGH]

### 1.6 Sponges in Homer
Hephaestus "with a sponge wiped he his face and his two hands withal, and his mighty neck and shaggy breast" (*Il.* 18.414–415, Murray). [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0134%3Abook%3D18%3Acard%3D394, HIGH] In Ithaca the suitors' servants "were washing the tables with porous sponges and setting them forth" (*Od.* 1.111–112, Murray). [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D1%3Acard%3D96, HIGH]

### 1.7 The old debate: why don't Homeric heroes eat fish?
- **Plato, *Republic* 404b–c** (Shorey): Socrates says one can learn the right soldier's diet "even from Homer. For you are aware that in the banqueting of the heroes on campaign he does not [404c] feast them on fish, nor on boiled meat, but only on roast, which is what soldiers could most easily procure." He goes on (404d) to reject "a Syracusan table and Sicilian variety of made dishes", "a little Corinthian maid", and "the seeming delights of Attic pastry." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0168%3Abook%3D3%3Asection%3D404c, HIGH]
- **Athenaeus** (2nd–3rd c. AD) frames the whole question at *Deipn.* 1.9 ff.: "Although Homer describes the Hellespont as teeming with fish, and pictures the Phaeacians as devoted to the sea, and although he knows that in Ithaca there are several harbours and many islands near the shore abounding in fish and wild fowl, and moreover counts the sea's bounty in supplying fish as an element of prosperity, he nevertheless never represents anyone as eating any of these creatures." (ToposText §1.16) And: "Homer never mentions such food in connexion with banquets, evidently because these viands were not considered appropriate to the heroes of high rank" (§1.22, listing the lost angling poets Numenius, Pancrates, Poseidonius, Oppian and prose writers Seleucus of Tarsus, Leonidas of Byzantium, Agathocles of Atrax). [https://topostext.org/work/218, HIGH] The "sea's bounty" allusion is to *Od.* 19.109–114, where the good king's sea "yields fish". [MEDIUM — the Odyssey line was not re-checked; Athenaeus's paraphrase is HIGH]
- Athenaeus also records the theory (4.45; the character quotes Meleager of Gadara) that Homer, "being a Syrian by birth, has represented the Achaeans as abstaining from fish according to the practice of his own country, although there is great abundance of them in the region of the Hellespont." [https://topostext.org/work/218, HIGH]
- The modern consensus reading: fish-eating is not "heroic" and belongs to a different register; John Wilkins puts it neatly — Archestratus's 4th-century poem "puts all the fish back into epic poetry that Homer had famously omitted from the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*." [https://antikmuseet.au.dk/fileadmin/www.antikmuseet.au.dk/Pontosfiler/BSS_2/BSS2_02_wilkins.pdf, HIGH] James Davidson (*Courtesans and Fishcakes*, 1997) treats the Homeric silence as the starting point for his history of Athenian fish-desire. [https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo11541590.html, MEDIUM]

## 2. Hesiod, *Works and Days* 618–694: the sailing calendar (a Boeotian's view of the sea)

Hesiod of Ascra (Boeotia, near Helicon) gives the earliest Greek sailing calendar. He is a farmer who distrusts the sea, and says nothing about fishing; but his seasonal rules governed every later coastal economy.

- **Close of the season (618–629):** "if desire for uncomfortable sea-faring seize you when the Pleiades plunge into the misty sea to escape Orion's rude strength, then truly gales of all kinds rage. Then keep ships no longer on the sparkling sea… Haul up your ship upon the land and pack it closely with stones all round… draw out the bilge-plug so that the rain of heaven may not rot it… hang up the well-shaped rudder over the smoke." (Evelyn-White; Perseus note dates the Pleiades' setting to late October/early November.) [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0132%3Acard%3D618, HIGH]
- **His father's emigration (633–640):** "he left Aeolian Cyme and fled, not from riches and substance, but from wretched poverty which Zeus lays upon men, and he settled near Helicon in a miserable hamlet, Ascra, which is bad in winter, sultry in summer, and good at no time." [same URL, HIGH]
- **Hesiod's only voyage (650–659):** "never yet have I sailed by ship over the wide sea, but only to Euboea from Aulis… I crossed over to Chalcis, to the games of wise Amphidamas… I gained the victory with a song and carried off a handled tripod." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0132%3Acard%3D641, HIGH]
- **The safe season (663–677):** "Fifty days after the solstice, when the season of wearisome heat is come to an end, is the right time for men to go sailing" — but "make all haste you can to return home again and do not wait till the time of the new wine and autumn rain." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0132%3Acard%3D663, HIGH]
- **Spring sailing and risk (678–694):** "Another time for men to go sailing is in spring when a man first sees leaves on the topmost shoot of a fig-tree as large as the foot-print that a crow makes… For my part I do not praise it… Yet in their ignorance men do even this, for wealth means life to poor mortals; but it is fearful to die among the waves… Do not put all your goods in hollow ships; leave the greater part behind, and put the lesser part on board… Observe due measure: and proportion is best in all things." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0132%3Acard%3D678, HIGH]

## 3. Archestratus of Gela, *Hedypatheia* ("Life of Luxury"), c. 350 BC

**The work.** A mock-epic gastronomic tour in hexameters, addressed to friends Moschus and Cleander; Athenaeus (7.278d–279a) says he "diligently traversed all lands and seas… testing carefully the delights of the belly" and imitated the writers of *Periploi*. About 62 fragments (c. 330 lines) survive, all in Athenaeus. Standard edition: S. D. Olson & A. Sens, *Archestratos of Gela: Greek Culture and Cuisine in the Fourth Century BCE* (Oxford 2000); an accessible translation is J. Wilkins & S. Hill, *Archestratus: Fragments from the Life of Luxury* (rev. ed. 2011). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archestratus, MEDIUM; https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.12.49/, MEDIUM; https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/4365016, HIGH for the edition's existence]

[https://www.loebclassics.com/view/atheneus_grammarian-learned_banqueters/2007/pb_LCL224.413.xml, MEDIUM (search snippet); Wilkins PDF above, HIGH] Other O–S numbers are **not given** below rather than guessed.

## 4. Aristotle: *Historia Animalium* and the Lesbos years

**Lesbos.** After Plato's death (347) Aristotle left Athens; he spent about two years on Lesbos (conventionally 345–343 BC) studying marine life, especially in the lagoon of Pyrrha (the modern Gulf of Kalloni), to which the *HA* refers repeatedly. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle%27s_biology, MEDIUM for the dates; https://www.lifewatch.be/aristotle-marine-linnaeus-avant-la-lettre, MEDIUM] Armand Leroi's *The Lagoon: How Aristotle Invented Science* (2014) is the modern popular account. [https://www.everand.com/book/769190854/The-Lagoon-How-Aristotle-Invented-Science, MEDIUM] Some scholars dispute whether the "Euripus of Pyrrha" is Kalloni. [https://www.academia.edu/17566127/The_Location_of_the_Euripus_of_Pyrrha_in_the_Works_of_Aristotle_and_Strabo, LOW — abstract only]

**Pyrrha in the text (Thompson translation):**
- *HA* 9.37 (621b): "Out of the lagoon of Pyrrha all the fishes swim in winter-time, except the sea-gudgeon; they swim out owing to the cold, for the narrow waters are colder than the outer sea." [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.9.ix.html, HIGH]
- *HA* 8.20 (603a): "In the Pyrrhaean Strait the clam was exterminated, partly by the dredging-machine used in their capture" — the earliest notice of over-fishing by dredge. [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.8.viii.html, HIGH]
- *HA* 5.15 (547a–b): purple-fish are large "off Sigeum and Lectum", small "in the Euripus, and on the coast of Caria". [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html, HIGH]

**Tuna migration (Book 8.13, 598a–b):** "The tunny proper, the pelamys, and the bonito penetrate into the Euxine in summer and pass the summer there… Tunny-fish swim into the Euxine keeping the shore on their right, and swim out of it with the shore upon their left. It is stated that they do so as being naturally weak-sighted, and seeing better with the right eye." [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.8.viii.html, HIGH] Athenaeus repeats the one-eyed tunny (7.301f–302a, "the tunny cannot see with the left eye, as Aristotle says") and Menander's *Fishermen*: "the miry sea, which feeds the mighty tunny". [ToposText §7.63, §7.66, HIGH] Twentieth-century Bosporus fishermen confirmed the counter-clockwise, right-shore migration. [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/4485254 (Çoruh Kurt 2025, citing Mylona 2021), MEDIUM]

**Tuna life history from fishermen's knowledge (Book 6.17, 571a):** "the fishermen infer this age from the circumstance that once when there was a failure of the young tunny fish for a year there was a failure of the full-grown tunny the next summer. They are of opinion that the tunny is a fish a year older than the pelamyd. The tunny and the mackerel pair about the close of the month of Elaphebolion, and spawn about the commencement of the month of Hecatombaeon… After the females have spawned in the Euxine, there comes from the egg what some call scordylae, but what the Byzantines nickname the 'auxids' or 'growers'… these fish go out of the Pontus in autumn along with the young tunnies, and enter Pontus in the spring as pelamyds." [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.6.vi.html, HIGH] Book 5 adds that the tunny breeds in the Euxine "and nowhere else". [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html, HIGH]

**Dolphins and fishermen (Book 4.9, 535b–536a):** "in the capture of the dolphin; for when the hunters have enclosed a shoal of these fishes with a ring of their canoes, they set up from inside the canoes a loud splashing in the water, and by so doing induce the creatures to run in a shoal high and dry up on the beach… Furthermore, when engaged in their craft, fishermen are particularly careful to make no noise with oar or net; and after they have spied a shoal, they let down their nets at a spot so far off that they count upon no noise being likely to reach the shoal… they adopt the stratagem of the dolphin-hunter; in other words they clatter stones together, that the fish may, in their fright, gather close into one spot, and so they envelop them within their nets." [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.4.iv.html, HIGH] (The famous dolphin chapter *HA* 9.48 on dolphins' affection for boys and for each other is well known but I could not retrieve its text — see §15.)

**Sponges (Book 5.16, 548a–549a):** three kinds — loose, close-textured, and "the sponge of Achilles" (finest); they grow "attached to a rock or on sea-beaches" and feed "in slime"; loose sponges "peculiarly abundant on the coast of Lycia"; sponges off Cape Malea differ by exposure. [https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html, HIGH]

**Murex (Book 5.15, 547a–b):** purple-fish gather "in the spring-time, and deposit the so-called 'honeycomb'"; fishermen catch them then, using creels; the dye "bloom" lies "between the mecon and the neck". [same URL, HIGH]

**Politics 1291b:** among "the people" Aristotle lists the maritime class — naval, trading, ferrying, fishing — noting "fishermen were particularly numerous at Tarentum and Byzantium", triremes at Athens, merchant shipping at Aegina and Chios, ferrymen at Tenedos. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D4%3Asection%3D1291b, HIGH]

## 5. Classical Athens: opson, the fish market, and comedy

**Opson / opsophagia.** *Opson* was originally any relish eaten with the cereal staple (*sitos*) but came to mean fish above all; the *opsophagos* is the fish-obsessive. Athenaeus (7.276e–277a, §7.4) says the term is used of "people who gad about among the fishmongers". [https://topostext.org/work/218, HIGH; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opson_and_sitos, MEDIUM] Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 3.14.2–4: Socrates calls a man "greedy" (*opsophagos*) who eats his *opson* without bread, "to tickle his palate", and asks the company to watch "whether he treats the bread as his meat or the meat as his bread." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0208%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D14, HIGH] Davidson's thesis: fresh fish was the luxury par excellence, its purchase a public act of conspicuous consumption in the agora; salt fish was the cheap alternative. [https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo11541590.html, MEDIUM; https://daily.jstor.org/fish-addiction-an-ancient-greek-paranoia/, MEDIUM] Wilkins's critique: Davidson's picture is "partial (he is interested only in the wealthy)"; fish "was accessible to all members of the ancient polis, depending on cost and supply". [Wilkins PDF, HIGH]

**The fish market and its fishmongers — Athenaeus 6.224b–228c, a run of Middle/New Comedy fragments:**
- Antiphanes, *Brave Lads*: "whenever I go to market… one glance there at the fishmongers, and I am straightway turned to stone… if I see what a small-sized fish it is for which they charge such a high price." (6.224c–d)
- Amphis, *Wandering Juggler*: easier "to get access to the General Staff" than "to approach the damned fishmongers"; the dealer "pounds a polyp" and clips his syllables: "'Twda cost y' eight pence [obols]." (6.224d–e)
- Alexis: "'How much are you offering those two mullets for,' he replies, 'Ten-pence.' 'Too steep! will you take eight?' 'Yes, if you will buy the one next to it.'" (6.224f–225a); "How in the world is it that they are not all rich, since they receive royal tributes?… take tithes of our property" (*Meeting at Pyale*, 6.226a); the "law" of Aristonicus that a fishmonger who sells below his first-named price is jailed (*Melting-Pot*, 6.226a–c).
- Xenarchus, *Purple-Shell*: fishmongers, forbidden by law to sprinkle water on their fish, stage a fake brawl so a bystander drenches the fish shouting "Water, water!" (6.225c–d).
- Diphilus, *Busybody*: an Athenian fishmonger quotes a sea-bass at "tenpence" and then "exacts the coin of Aigina" (6.225b); *Merchant*: "I don't remember ever seeing fish dearer. Great Poseidon! If thou didst day by day receive a tithe of their cost, thou wouldst be richest of the gods." (c. 6.227e)
- Antiphanes, *Knave-Hater*: fishmongers the "foulest breed" — "only after the money-lenders" (6.226e–f).
- Athenaeus adds that the council chose "fish-inspectors" (*opsonomoi*) and that Lynceus of Samos wrote a *How to Buy in the Market* against "the murderous fishmongers" (6.228c–d).
[https://topostext.org/work/218 §§6.4–6.12, HIGH for quotations; Casaubon pages MEDIUM]

**Prices (comic, so exaggerated):** Copaic eel 3 drachmas (Ar. *Ach.* 962, 425 BC); Eupolis, *Flatterers*: "For only a hundred drachmas I have bought fish — eight sea-bass and twelve gilt-heads" (7.328b); Menander: gobies priced at "four drachmas… too much" (7.309e); a huge piece of skinless salt-fish "worth a drachma; for an obol I bought it" (Nicostratus/Philetaerus, 3.118e). [ToposText, HIGH] Chrysippus: "In Athens they despise small fry on account of their abundance, and declare that they are beggars' food" (7.285c–d). [HIGH] The most important non-literary price document is the Hellenistic fish-price decree from Akraiphia in Boeotia, edited by E. Lytle, "Fish Lists in the Wilderness", *Hesperia* 79 (2010) 253–303. [https://www.academia.edu/2132459/, MEDIUM — not read]

**Fishermen vs. generals:** Anaxandrides, *Odysseus*: Athenaeus says "fishermen take more pride than the most eminent generals in their profession" (6.227b–c). Alexis, *Odysseus at the Loom*: "let me cast the fishermen, too, into the pit; they catch for me only fish fit for freedmen, bony anchovies, little cuttle-fish, and some small fry" (7.303a–b). [HIGH]

**The Iasos bell (Caria, not Athens, but the best fish-market anecdote):** Strabo 14.2.21: at Iasos "the people gain most of their livelihood from the sea, for the sea here is well supplied with fish, but the soil of the country is rather poor"; a citharode's audience walked out "when the bell that announced the sale of fish rang", except one deaf man, who on being told the bell had rung said "Fare thee well" and left too. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/14B*.html, HIGH]

## 6. Techniques and gear, with primary sources

**Homer's kit (8th c.):** rod (*Od.* 12.251), line and bronze hook (*Il.* 16.408), ox-horn guard (*Il.* 24.81; *Od.* 12.253), lead sinker (*Il.* 24.80), bait for "little fishes" (*Od.* 12.252), bent hooks (*Od.* 4.369), beach seine (*Od.* 22.384–387). [§1 URLs, HIGH]

**Plato's taxonomy of fishing, *Sophist* 220a–221c (c. 360 BC):** water-hunting divides into enclosure-hunting (nets, weels etc.) and striking; striking "which takes place at night by the light of a fire is, I suppose, called by the hunters themselves fire-hunting" (220d), while daytime striking "is, as a whole, barb-hunting, since the spears, as well as the hooks, are tipped with barbs" (220d); tridents strike downward from above, whereas angling hooks the head and mouth and draws the fish "up from below with rods and reeds" (220e–221a) — the definition of *aspalieutike*, angling. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0172%3Atext%3DSoph.%3Asection%3D220d, HIGH for 220d; MEDIUM for the exact wording at 220e–221a]

**Plato, *Laws* 823d–824a:** the ideal young citizen should feel no "desire or craving for hunting by sea, or for angling, or for ever pursuing water-animals with creels that do your lazy hunting for you, whether you sleep or wake" (823d–e); the final law: "The fisherman shall be allowed to hunt in all waters except havens and sacred rivers and pools and lakes, but only on condition that he makes no use of muddying juices" (824a) — i.e. no fish-poisons. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0166%3Abook%3D7%3Asection%3D823e, HIGH]

**Aristotle:** creels for murex (*HA* 5.15); dredge for clams at Pyrrha (8.20); nets set at a distance, silence, then noise to drive fish (4.9); a shoal "basking at the surface" approached silently to gauge its size (4.9). [§4 URLs, HIGH]

**Hellenistic epigrams (3rd c. BC) — the fullest inventories:**
- Leonidas of Tarentum, *AP* 6.4: Diophantus dedicates to Poseidon "his hook, easily gulped down, his long poles, his line, his creels, this weel, device of sea-faring net-fishers, his sharp trident, weapon of Poseidon, and the two oars of his boat." [https://www.attalus.org/poetry/leonidas.html, HIGH]
- Anon., *AP* 6.23 (to Hermes): "this fragment of the great seine worn by the sea… this little purse-seine, the round weel that entraps fishes, the float whose task it is to mark where the weels are concealed, and the long cane rod, the child of the marsh, with its horse-hair line, not unfurnished with hooks." [https://www.attalus.org/poetry/anth6.html, HIGH]
- Satyrius, *AP* 6.11: three brothers dedicate to Pan — "Damis the huntsman this long net, Pigres his light-meshed fowling net, and Clitor, the night-rower, his tunic [net] for red mullet"; Leonidas 6.13 varies it: Cleitor's net "from the denizens of the deep… through the shore-water." [same URLs, HIGH] "Night-rower" is night fishing.
- Maccius, *AP* 6.33: "Priapus of the beach, the fishermen, after surrounding with their deep-sunk net the circling shoal of tunnies in the green narrows of the sea, dedicated to you…" — a Hellenistic/early-Imperial tuna seine. [attalus anth6, HIGH]
- Theocritus (or pseudo-), *Idyll* 21: two old fishermen in a wattled hut on dry seaweed; beside them "small baskets, reeds, hooks with seaweed-covered bait, lines, wicker traps, and oars. The sea pressed close to their tender hut." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0228%3Atext%3DId.%3Apoem%3D21, HIGH; authorship MEDIUM — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idyll_XXI]
- Leonidas, *AP* 7.295: Theris "who got his living from his lucky weels… the seine-hauler, the prober of crevices in the rocks"; 7.504: Parmis "the shore-fisher, a first class hand at catching wrasse and scarus and the perch… near his lines, rod, and hooks." [attalus leonidas, HIGH]

**Comedy:** Eubulus: "I let down a shrimp and pulled it up again" (line-fishing with shrimp bait, Ath. 3.105f); Araros: shrimps "leaped… like dolphins into the rope-twined pot" (a rope weel, 3.105f). [ToposText §3.66, HIGH] Aristotle in Athenaeus: the parrot-fish "likes to feed on seaweed, and therefore can be caught with it", and "cannot be caught even at night" (7.319e–320a). [HIGH]

**Night fishing with fire.** Plato's "fire-hunting" (above); Satyrius's "night-rower"; and Oppian *Hal.* 4.635–646: "Then do the fishes exulting in the oily flame of pine rush about the boat and, to their sorrow seeing the fire at even, meet the stern blow of the trident." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/4*.html, HIGH] E. Lytle, "Fishing with Fire: Technology, Economy and Two Greek Inscriptions", *Historia* 67 (2018) 61–102, argues from two Hellenistic/Imperial inscriptions that fire-fishing was large-scale and drove a trade in torchwood. [https://philpapers.org/rec/LYTFWF, MEDIUM]

**Fish poison.** Oppian 4.647–680: a paste of white clay and cyclamen root kneaded into cakes and spread at fish caves, so the fish flee into nets. [Oppian bk 4 URL, HIGH] Compare Plato's ban on "muddying juices" (*Laws* 824a).

**Oppian's *Halieutica* — the fullest ancient fishing manual (Roman period).** Written c. AD 177–180 by a Greek poet of Cilicia and dedicated to "Antoninus" (Marcus Aurelius, with Commodus); five books, c. 3,500 hexameters. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppian, MEDIUM; https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/1*.html, HIGH for the dedication "will I declare, O Antoninus"] Because it is 500 years after Archestratus it must be used with care for the Classical Aegean, but its techniques are conservative. Key passages:
- *Hal.* 1.28–56 contrasts the hunter, who fights "securely on the land", with fishermen whose "labours are uncertain… always they have to encounter the chill and wildly raging water", armed only with "feeble hairs and bent hooks of bronze and… nets". [bk 1 URL, HIGH]
- *Hal.* 3.72–91, the four modes: "Some delight in Hooks; and of these some fish with a well-twisted line of horse-hair fastened to long reeds, others simply cast a flaxen cord attached to their hands, another rejoices in landed lines or in line with many hooks. Others prefer to array Nets; and of these there are those called casting-nets, and those called draw-nets — drag-nets and round bag-nets and seines. Others they call cover-nets, and, with the seines, there are those called ground-nets and ball-nets and the crooked trawl. Others again have their minds set rather upon Weels which bring joy to their masters while they sleep at ease. Others with the long pronged Trident wound the fish from land or from a ship as they will." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/3*.html, HIGH]
- *Hal.* 3.620–648, the tuna watch: "There first a skilful Tunny-watcher ascends a steep high hill, who remarks the various shoals, their kind and size, and informs his comrades. Then straightway all the nets are set forth in the waves like a city." [same URL, HIGH]
- *Hal.* 5.612–674, the sponge-divers; 5.416–447, dolphin-hunting condemned (§9, §11 below).

**Aelian, *On Animals* (c. AD 200, Greek, Roman period):** 15.1, the Macedonian fly on the Astraeus: "They wrap the hook in scarlet wool, and to the wool they attach two feathers that grow beneath a cock's wattles and are the colour of wax" — the first artificial fly in literature; 15.5, the tuna watcher "like a general he gives the signal, or like a conductor, the keynote", five boats in line each dropping its section of net, "as though it were a captured city"; 15.6, prayers to Poseidon at the tuna catch. [https://www.attalus.org/translate/animals15.html, HIGH]

**Hooks: bronze then iron.** Homer's hooks are bronze (*Il.* 16.408); Oppian still says "bent hooks of bronze" (1.53) though iron hooks are common archaeologically by then. For the Early Iron Age archaeology (hooks, weights, fish bone from Euboean-Gulf sites such as Lefkandi/Kynos) see T. Theodoropoulou, "Fishing in dark waters" (2011). [https://www.academia.edu/17656180/, MEDIUM — abstract only; the bronze-to-iron transition is not documented here in detail]

## 7. Tuna: watchtowers, the Bosporus fishery, coins, religion

**Migration as the Greeks understood it:** Aristotle *HA* 8.13 and 6.17 (§4). Strabo 12.3.11 (Sinope) and 12.3.19 (Pharnacia): the pelamydes are "first caught" at Pharnacia/Trapezus, "the Sinopeans get the second catch and the Byzantians the third." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/12C*.html, HIGH]

**Byzantium and the Golden Horn — Strabo 7.6.2:** the Horn runs c. 60 stadia and has bays where pelamydes are "even caught by hand"; they are born in Lake Maeotis, run along the Asian shore, and at the Cyaneae "a certain white rock which projects from the Chalcedonian shore" frightens them across to the Horn, "providing the Byzantines and the Roman people considerable revenue"; the Chalcedonians got none, which is why the oracle called them "blind" for choosing the poorer shore. [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0198%3Abook%3D7%3Achapter%3D6%3Asection%3D2, HIGH] Aristotle *Pol.* 1291b: fishermen "particularly numerous at… Byzantium". [§4, HIGH] Archestratus fr. 38: Byzantium the tunny's "mother-city"; the anonymous "Hesiodic" verses quoted by Euthydemus: "of tunnies, pickled in the right season, Byzantium is mother, as well as of deep-sea mackerel and well-fed swordfish, while Parium Town is a glorious nurse of Spanish mackerel" (Ath. 3.116b–c). [ToposText §3.84, HIGH] Hicesius: "a great difference in the horaia of Byzantium and those caught in other places… true not of tunny alone, but of all other fishes taken in Byzantium" (3.116e–f). [HIGH]

**Thynnoskopeion (tuna watch-station).** Strabo 5.2.6 (Populonia): "Beneath the promontory there is a place for watching the tunny-fish"; 5.2.8 (Cosa): "a station for observing the tunny-fish; for along the shore the tunny-fish follow not only the acorns but also the purple fish." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/5B*.html, HIGH] Oppian 3.620 ff. and Aelian 15.5 (above). Hellenistic and early-Imperial inscriptions from Kyzikos and Parion record rented tuna watchtowers and fishing partnerships: a 1st-c. BC stele from Kyzikos dedicated to Poseidon and Artemis/Aphrodite Pontia names a partnership of eleven with a rented watchtower and roles such as fish-watchers, boatmen, net-pullers; a Parion inscription (1st–2nd c. AD) has a partnership of more than thirty; a Kos inscription records the sale of the right to tax the watchtowers. [https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/4485254 (Çoruh Kurt, *Oannes* 7.1, 2025, 187–203), MEDIUM — secondary, citing Felic 2017, Marzano 2018, Lytle 2012] Aelian describes the watch-post as two pine poles leaning seaward. [same, MEDIUM]

**Tuna on coins — Kyzikos.** From c. 600–550 BC Kyzikos (Propontis, a Milesian colony) struck electrum staters and fractions with a tunny as civic badge beneath an ever-changing main type — over 200 obverse types across three centuries (von Fritze's catalogue; Hurter & Liewald). Wildwinds examples: EL hemihekte c. 500–460 "Crab; tunny right above"; EL hekte c. 500–475 "Facing lion's head; tunny below" (BMC Mysia 38); EL stater c. 460–400 "Triton left, holding tunny in right hand; tunny below" (Hurter 26); EL hekte c. 400 "Dionysus reclining… tunny fish below" (von Fritze 155). [https://www.wildwinds.com/coins/greece/mysia/kyzikos/t.html, HIGH] The Boston MFA holds a stater with "Poseidon on hippocamp above tunny fish". [https://collections.mfa.org/objects/1938, MEDIUM — page blocked; title from search] Çoruh Kurt: "the oldest tuna coins were minted in Kyzikos"; the tunny "was used on Kyzikos coins for centuries" (600–300 BC in electrum), later on silver/bronze; other tuna-coin cities include Gades and Sicilian mints. [dergipark URL, MEDIUM]

**Tuna in cult.** Antigonus of Carystus (3rd c. BC), *On Diction*: "the Halaians, when they celebrate a festival to Poseidon in the tunny season, offer to the god in the event of a good catch the first tunny caught; and this offering is called a *thynnaion*" (Ath. 7.297e; repeated 7.303a). Halai is almost certainly the coastal Attic deme Halai Aixonides. [ToposText §7.50, §7.66, HIGH; deme identification MEDIUM] Cleanthes of Corinth's painting in the sanctuary of Artemis Alpheiosa near Olympia showed "Poseidon… offering a tunny to Zeus, who is in labour" (Ath. 8.346b–c). [§8.36, HIGH] Aelian 15.6: fishermen pray to Poseidon at the tuna catch. [attalus, HIGH] Hipponax (6th c.): a glutton "feasting undisturbed and noisily on tunny and an olio every day" (Ath. 7.304b). [HIGH]

**Economy.** Polybius 4.38.4–5: from the Pontus come "most plentiful supplies and best qualities of cattle and slaves… honey, wax, and preserved fish", against exported olive oil and wine; corn goes both ways. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/4*.html, HIGH]

## 8. Salt fish (*tarichos*) and the Pontic trade

- **Earliest Athenian evidence:** Hermippus, *Phormophoroi* (c. 420s BC): "from the Hellespont mackerel and all kinds of salt-dried fish" among Athens's imports (Ath. 1.27e–28a). [ToposText §1.49, HIGH]
- **Comic imports:** Antiphanes, *Deucalion*: "Salt sturgeon, if one likes it, or a Gadeira tunny; and revels in the odour of a roe tunny from Byzantium"; Nicostratus/Philetaerus: "Let a Byzantian fish-slice come to our revels, and let a Gadeira belly-slice enter beside it" (3.118d–e). [§3.88, HIGH]
- **Athenaeus's salt-fish section is 3.116a–121e** — grades (*horaia*, cubes, *saperdes*, *kybion*), sources (Pontus, Byzantium, Parion, Gadeira, Sicily, Sardinia, Spain, Egypt), and the physicians Hicesius, Diphilus of Siphnos, Mnesitheus. [Wilkins PDF, HIGH; ToposText, HIGH]
- **Archestratus fr. 39 O–S** (above) despises Pontic *saperdes* but praises Byzantine *horaion* and lightly cured mackerel. [HIGH]
- **Polybius 4.38** (above) for Pontic salt fish as a staple export. [HIGH]
- **Status:** salt fish was the cheap, everyday protein, fresh fish the luxury; Dicaeopolis tells the general Lamachus "Let him eat salt fish, while he shakes his plumes" (*Ach.* 967). [https://topostext.org/work/57, HIGH]
- **Archaeology:** Punic salt-fish amphorae with fish bone in the "Punic Amphora Building" at Corinth (5th c. BC) attest western *tarichos* reaching mainland Greece. [https://www.academia.edu/19900611/, MEDIUM — abstract] For production economics in the Aegean see E. Lytle, "The Economics of Saltfish Production in the Aegean during the Classical and Hellenistic Periods", *J. Maritime Archaeology* 13 (2018) 407–418. [https://academia.edu/37949331/, MEDIUM — abstract]
- Even *tarichoi* could be sacrificed: the Phaselites offered preserved fish because their founder Lacius bought the site with salt fish (Ath. 7.297f–298a). [§7.51, HIGH]

## 9. Sponge diving

- **Homer:** sponges for washing tables and bodies (*Il.* 18.414; *Od.* 1.111). [§1.6, HIGH]
- **Aristotle *HA* 5.16:** sponge kinds, habitats, Lycia and Cape Malea (§4). Aristotle's remark that the best sponges come from deep water is usually taken to imply diving. [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1439-0485.2008.00235.x (Pronzato & Manconi 2008), MEDIUM] The often-repeated claim that Aristotle describes an "elephant's trunk" breathing tube for divers comes from *Problemata* 32.5 (a text of doubtful authenticity), not *HA*. [same review, LOW]
- **Plato:** modern surveys say Plato mentions the sponge as a bathing article; I could not locate the passage. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_diving, LOW]
- **Oppian *Hal.* 5.612–674** (Roman period) is the classic description: the sponge-cutter, disciplined in diet, takes a lead weight and a sickle, holds oil in his mouth and spits it out to light the bottom, cuts the sponge quickly, and jerks the rope to be hauled up; "hateful blood is sprinkled straightway from the Sponges" and sea-monsters sometimes take the diver. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Oppian/Halieutica/5*.html, HIGH]
- **Kalymnos and Symi (preview of the modern tradition):** Kalymnos, where only c. 18% of the land could be farmed, became the Aegean's sponge centre; divers used the *skandalopetra*, a c. 15 kg stone on a rope, working naked at c. 30 m for up to 5 minutes; the surface-supplied *skafandro* suit arrived in 1865 and caused mass deaths from decompression sickness (one French naval physician recorded 10 deaths among 24 divers in 1867; estimates of c. 10,000 Mediterranean diver deaths in fifty years). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_diving, MEDIUM; https://www.greeka.com/dodecanese/kalymnos/history/sponge-diving-tradition/, LOW]

## 10. Purple dye (murex) as a marine industry

- **Aristotle *HA* 5.15**: spring gathering, "honeycomb" spawn, capture in creels, the dye gland; large murex off Sigeum and Lectum (Troad), small in the Euripus and Caria. [§4, HIGH]
- **Hermione (Argolid).** Plutarch, *Alexander* 36 (Perrin): at Susa Alexander found 40,000 talents of coin and "five thousand talents' weight of purple from Hermione, which, although it had been stored there for a hundred and ninety years, still kept its colours fresh and lively… honey was used in the purple dyes, and white olive oil in the white dyes." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Alexander*/5.html, HIGH] (190 years before 331 BC is c. 520 BC — an Archaic Hermionian export to the Persian court.) Nearby Halieis, "the Fishermen", was a port of Hermionis whose "townsfolk derived their name from their fisheries" (Strabo 8.6.11 = C373; Pausanias 2.36.1 knows it as deserted "Halice"). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halieis, MEDIUM; https://topostext.org/work/213, HIGH for Pausanias]
- **Laconia and Kythera.** Pausanias 3.21.6: "the best shell-fish for the manufacture of purple dye after those of the Phoenician sea are to be found on the coast of Laconia." [https://topostext.org/work/213, HIGH] Pliny *NH* 9.60: "In Asia the best purple is that of Tyre, in Africa that of Meninx… and in Europe that of Laconia." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D9%3Achapter%3D60, HIGH] Pliny *NH* 4.19 (4.56): "Cythera, with the town of that name, the former name of which island was Porphyris"; Bostock's note: "It derived its name of Porphyris from the purple fishery established here by the Phoenicians." [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D4%3Achapter%3D19, HIGH for Pliny; the Phoenician attribution is the translator's note, LOW] The variant "Porphyrousa" attributed to Aristotle (via Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Kythera) is widely repeated but I could not check the entry. [https://topostext.org/place/363230PKyt, LOW] "Cerigo" is the Venetian name of Kythera. [Pliny/Bostock note, HIGH]
- **Xenarchus's comedy *Purple-Shell*** (Ath. 6.225c) and Timocles' fisherman "of consummate skill" in the same play (7.319a) show the murex-gatherer as a stock figure in 4th-c. Athens. [ToposText, HIGH]

## 11. Fishing in religion and myth

- **Poseidon**: patron of fishers — Leonidas *AP* 6.4 ("weapon of Poseidon" for the trident); Epicharmus, *Marriage of Hebe*: "Poseidon, that leader of the fisherfolk, came in person, bringing, in Phoenician barques, the fairest sea-breams and parrot-fishes" (Ath. 7.320e); the *thynnaion* first-tuna offering; Diphilus's tithe joke; Aelian 15.6; the Kyzikos fishermen's stele to Poseidon and Aphrodite Pontia. [§6, §7 URLs, HIGH/MEDIUM]
- **Glaucus of Anthedon (Boeotia).** Pausanias 9.22.6–7 (Jones): at Anthedon "by the sea is what is called the Leap of Glaucus. That Glaucus was a fisherman, who, on eating of the grass, turned into a deity of the sea and ever since has foretold to men the future, is a belief generally accepted; in particular, seafaring men tell every year many a tale about the soothsaying of Glaucus. Pindar and Aeschylus got a story about Glaucus from the people of Anthedon… the story actually supplied Aeschylus with material for a play" (the lost *Glaucus Pontios*). [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/xmlchunk?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0160%3Abook%3D9%3Achapter%3D22, HIGH] Ovid, *Met.* 13.904 ff. tells the herb story and Glaucus's love for Scylla. [https://www.theoi.com/Pontios/Glaukos.html, MEDIUM — page blocked; reference from search] Archestratus's hake "which Anthedon nurtures" (7.316a) shows the town's fishery in the 4th c. [HIGH]
- **Sacred fish and taboos**: Pancrates' *pompilos* (pilot-fish) "sacred" to Poseidon and the Samothracian gods, with the punished fisherman Epopeus of Icarus (Ath. 7.283a–c); the Boeotian eel sacrifice (7.297c–d); red mullet offered to Hecate at Aexone (7.325e). [HIGH] Homer's "sacred fish" (*Il.* 16.407). [HIGH]
- **Dedications by fishermen**: *AP* 6.4 (Poseidon), 6.23 (Hermes), 6.11–16 (Pan), 6.33 and 6.89 (Priapus), 6.196 (Pan, a crab as first-fruits), 6.230 (Apollo, a trumpet-shell). [attalus anth6/leonidas, HIGH]
- **Delian Apollo and Delos's poverty**: in the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* Leto promises Delos "all men will bring you hecatombs… you will feed those who dwell in you from the hand of strangers; for truly your own soil is not rich" (58–60); Delos fears Apollo will scorn her "for truly I have but a hard, rocky soil" and that "Many-footed creatures of the sea will make their lairs in me and black seals their dwellings undisturbed, because I lack people" (72–77). [https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0138%3Ahymn%3D3%3Acard%3D47, HIGH] Athenaeus also knows an "Apollo Opsophagos (Gourmand) worshipped in Elis" (8.346b). [HIGH]
- **Dolphins**: Aristotle on dolphin drives (*HA* 4.9); Duris of Samos: a dolphin at Iasos fell in love with the boy Dionysius and carried him out to sea and back (Ath. 13.606c–d). [ToposText §13.85, HIGH] Oppian 5.416–447: "The hunting of Dolphins is immoral", the dolphin-hunter can no more approach the gods "as a welcome sacrificer"; Thracians hunt calves so that mothers follow and both die. [Oppian bk 5 URL, HIGH]

## 12. Fishermen (*halieis*): status, communities, associations

- **Low status, hard life.** Plato would bar the young from angling and from "creels that do your lazy hunting for you" (*Laws* 823e), leaving fishing legal but unpraised (824a). [HIGH] Oppian 1.28–56 on fishermen's uncertain toil. [HIGH] Theocritus 21: "Poverty alone awakens the arts, Diophantus; it is the teacher of toil"; Asphalion dreams of a golden fish and is told to "seek the real flesh-and-blood fish, lest sleeping you die of hunger." [Perseus Theocritus, HIGH] Leonidas' epitaphs (7.295, 7.504) — reed huts, deaths by fishbone. [HIGH] Alexis: fishermen catch "fish fit for freedmen". [HIGH] Antiphanes' *Etruscan* jokes that a man from the deme Halae will bring "a Thracian wife or a plaice or a lamprey, or some damned big thing from the sea" (7.329a). [HIGH]
- **Pride and numbers.** Anaxandrides' fisherman boasts his art beats the painters' (Ath. 6.227b–c); Aristotle counts fishermen as a distinct demographic mass at Tarentum and Byzantium (*Pol.* 1291b). [HIGH]
- **Associations.** Leonidas *AP* 7.295 (3rd c. BC): Theris's tomb "was not set up by his children or wife, but by the guild of his fellow fishermen" — the earliest literary hint of a fishermen's collective. [attalus leonidas, HIGH; the word "guild" is Paton's rendering, MEDIUM] Epigraphic associations are late Hellenistic and Roman: the Kyzikos partnership stele (1st c. BC); the Ephesos fishery toll-office dedicated by "the fishermen and fish-dealers" (AD 54–59, *IEph* 20); a Kyzikos grave with fines payable to the fishermen; a Parion dedication to Priapos "by fellow-sailors and fishermen" (1st–2nd c. AD); fishermen honouring Ulpius Karpos at Miletos (c. AD 130). [https://philipharland.com/greco-roman-associations/?s=fishermen, MEDIUM; dergipark URL, MEDIUM]
- **Regulation of the sea.** Lytle argues Greek poleis generally treated the open sea as common (*thalassa koine*) and taxed the *market* rather than the catch. [https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1525/ca.2012.31.1.1 (Lytle, *Classical Antiquity* 31, 2012), MEDIUM — abstract]
- **Halieis, a town named "Fishermen"**: see §10 (Argolid). [MEDIUM]

## 13. Delos as Hellenistic hub — and its fish

- **Sacred poverty**: *Hymn to Apollo* 58–77 (above). [HIGH]
- **Free port and slave market**: Strabo 10.5.4: "the importers changed their business to Delos because they were attracted both by the immunity which the temple enjoyed and by the convenient situation of the harbour"; "The general festival is a kind of commercial affair, and it was frequented by Romans more than by any other people, even when Corinth was still in existence"; the razing of Corinth (146 BC) "increased its fame still more"; Mithridates' generals "completely ruined it" (88 BC). Rheneia, four stadia away, is the burial isle. [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/10E*.html, HIGH] Strabo 14.5.2: Delos "could both admit and send away ten thousand slaves on the same day", whence the proverb "Merchant, sail in, unload your ship, everything has been sold." [https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/14E*.html, HIGH]
- **Fish and the Delian economy.** E. Lytle, "'Entirely Ignorant of the Agora' (Alkiphron 1.14.3): Fishing and the Economy of Hellenistic Delos", in Ager & Faber (eds.), *Belonging and Isolation in the Hellenistic World* (Toronto 2013) 295–315: the Delian accounts record a *dekate* (tithe) on fish yielding 1,850 drachmas in 250 BC, implying a substantial fishery; Lytle estimates annual seafood consumption of roughly 18–36 kg per head on the island. [https://www.academia.edu/3391001/, MEDIUM — abstract and summary only; figures not independently checked] I found no explicit ancient attestation of a Delian *ichthyopolion* (fish-market building); the fish tax is the evidence for a regulated fish trade.
- **Freshwater fish in the Sacred Lake**: a 2019/2020 archaeozoological study (*J. Archaeological Science: Reports*) reports the first freshwater fish bones from Hellenistic Delos and argues fish were deliberately acclimatized/managed in the Sacred Lake. [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X19304158, MEDIUM — page blocked; from search abstract]
- The Renaissance map-name "Sdiles" for Delos is presumably an Italian corruption of "(i)s Dilos"; I could not verify it. [LOW]
