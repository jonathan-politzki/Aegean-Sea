# The Earliest Fishermen and Sailors of the Aegean
## Palaeolithic to the end of the Bronze Age (c. 40,000 – 1100 BC)

A sourced dossier. Every bullet carries a source URL and a confidence rating:

- **HIGH** = stated in a peer-reviewed paper, excavation monograph, museum catalogue, or the excavator's own report, and read (or its abstract read) directly.
- **MEDIUM** = from a reliable secondary source (university teaching site, encyclopaedia, museum-adjacent site, search-engine excerpt of a scholarly paper I could not open in full), or where sources disagree.
- **LOW** = plausible and widely repeated but resting on a popular or unverifiable source.

Where sources disagree I give both. Where I could not confirm something I say so (see the final section). Dates are calibrated BC unless flagged "uncal."

Map-name equivalences supplied by the requester for the 1683 chart (Milo = Melos, St. Erini = Thera/Santorini, Candie = Crete, Zea = Kea, Stalimene = Lemnos, Sira = Syros, Paro = Paros, Fermenia/Thermia = Kythnos, Pelagnisi = Kyra Panagia/Pelagos) are repeated as given; I verified only that Kyra Panagia is also called Pelagos / Pelagonisi today (see §2).

## 0. The short version

- People were crossing open water in the Aegean by the **11th millennium BC** (obsidian from Melos reaches Franchthi Cave on the mainland), and possibly earlier in the Late Pleistocene.
- By the **9th millennium BC** there were fishing communities living *on the islands* (Maroulas on Kythnos; the Cave of the Cyclops on Youra), using **bone fish-hooks**.
- In the **8th millennium BC** the Franchthi foragers ran an episode of intensive **bluefin tuna** fishing.
- In the **5th millennium BC** a Neolithic hamlet on Saliagos (between Paros and Antiparos) left a fish-bone assemblage that is **~97 % tuna**, apparently taken without hooks.
- By the **3rd millennium BC** Syriot craftsmen were incising **oared longboats with fish emblems** on "frying pans"; by the **mid-2nd millennium BC** the Therans painted **fishermen with strings of dolphinfish** and a **flotilla among leaping dolphins**, and the Minoans were casting **bronze hooks**, weighting nets with **lead**, and crushing **murex** for purple.

## 1. Franchthi Cave, Argolid (Peloponnese) — the anchor sequence

### The site and its excavators
- Franchthi is a large cave on the Argolid coast opposite Koilada; its deposits (over 11 m deep in Trench F/A) run discontinuously from the Upper Palaeolithic (c. 35,000+ years ago) through the Mesolithic to the end of the Neolithic (c. 3000 BC). [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, HIGH] [https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/franchthi-cave, MEDIUM]
- Excavated by **Thomas W. Jacobsen** (Indiana University). Sources differ on the start year: 1967–1976 [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, HIGH; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franchthi_Cave, MEDIUM] versus 1968–1976 [https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/franchthi-cave, MEDIUM]. Jacobsen's synthesis: "Franchthi Cave and the Beginning of Settled Village Life in Greece," *Hesperia* 50.4 (1981). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franchthi_Cave, MEDIUM]
- **Catherine Perlès** (CNRS) published the lithic industries and, in 2016, the synthesis of littoral resource use: Perlès, C., "Food and ornaments: Diachronic changes in the exploitation of littoral resources at Franchthi Cave (Argolid, Greece) during the Upper Palaeolithic and the Mesolithic (39,000–7000 cal BC)," *Quaternary International* 407 (2016) 45–58. [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1040618215005534, HIGH (citation); abstract via https://hal.science/hal-01529062/, HIGH]
- **Judith Powell**'s fish-bone study is *Fishing in the Prehistoric Aegean* (Studies in Mediterranean Archaeology and Literature, Pocket-book 137; Jonsered: Paul Åströms Förlag, 1996); it draws heavily on the Franchthi fish bones and argues Aegean fishing proper begins in the 8th millennium BC. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/environment/fish-and-shells/, HIGH (citation); https://www.namuseum.gr/en/monthly_artefact/the-return-of-the-fishermen/, MEDIUM]

### The Mesolithic phases and the tuna episode
- Phase dates: Lower Mesolithic c. 8700–7900 BC; Upper Mesolithic c. 7900–7500 BC. [https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/franchthi-cave, MEDIUM] (The Dartmouth site gives the older uncalibrated scheme: Lower Mesolithic c. 8300–7000 "b.c.", Upper Mesolithic c. 7000–6000 "b.c." — uncal.) [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, MEDIUM]
- Perlès 2016 abstract: fish were captured at Franchthi "since at least the 13th millennium cal BC," and there was "an episode of intense tuna fishing in the Upper Mesolithic (8th millennium cal BC)." [https://hal.science/hal-01529062/ (abstract quoted in search results), HIGH]
- Stiner & Munro, "On the evolution of diet and landscape during the Upper Paleolithic through Mesolithic at Franchthi Cave," *Journal of Human Evolution* 60 (2011) 618–636: marine fishing "initially focused on inshore breams, but the importance of these fish was overtaken with time by barracuda and eventually tunny. By the Upper Mesolithic, foragers had a full suite of technology that allowed them to fish the open waters of the Mediterranean for large-bodied taxa such as tunny." [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21371735/, HIGH]
- In the Upper Mesolithic, "fish bones increase in number to ca. 20–40 % of the total bone assemblage, and these fish are mainly large … probably tuna, for the most part." [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, HIGH]
- Species in the *Lower* Mesolithic were still shallow-water fish (eels, sea breams, mullets); in the *Upper* Mesolithic "tuna remains dominate the fish assemblage." Martinoia et al., *PLoS ONE* 20(1): e0310834 (2025). [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0310834, HIGH]
- **A caution worth repeating to a fisherman:** the Dartmouth synthesis notes that the *number of individual* large fish is actually small, and that tuna "might well have been herded into shallow water and clubbed or speared, so their bones need not imply deep-sea fishing." [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, HIGH] Wikipedia likewise offers both shore-netting and open-water capture as possibilities. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franchthi_Cave, MEDIUM]
- **A second caution:** compound-specific isotope analysis of Lower Mesolithic and Middle Neolithic human bone from Franchthi shows "little or no consumption of marine foods" in those individuals — but no Upper Mesolithic (tuna-phase) individuals were available to test. [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0310834, HIGH]
- Bluefin tuna vertebrae from Franchthi and Saliagos are cited as evidence that "by the Neolithic, BFT was already being caught at some scale" in the Aegean, with concentrated finds across the Aegean "between 9000 and 3200 years BCE." Andrews et al., *ICES Journal of Marine Science* 79(2) (2022) 247–262. [https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/79/2/247/6511216, HIGH]

### Melian obsidian and the earliest proof of seafaring
- Obsidian at Franchthi was sourced to **Melos**, about 150 km to the south-east across open water; it occurs already in Upper Palaeolithic levels in small quantities. [https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/franchthi-cave, MEDIUM]
- The most conservative statement (Perlès 1987, as quoted by Laskaris et al. 2011): the "oldest securely dated material comes from the eleventh millennium cal BC, lithic phase VI." Laskaris, Sampson, Mavridis & Liritzis, "Late Pleistocene/Early Holocene seafaring in the Aegean: new obsidian hydration dates with the SIMS-SS method," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 38.9 (2011) 2475–2479. [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440311001798, HIGH (citation); quotation via search excerpt of https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/AUTHORS/Laskaris2011-Obsidian.pdf, MEDIUM]
- The same paper describes the Franchthi obsidian as "a few pieces … dating to the end of the Upper Palaeolithic (ca. 35,000–11,000 B.P.)," with use continuing through the Mesolithic (c. 9600–6800 BC) and rising to about 3 % of the lithics. [https://novoscriptorium.com/2020/12/03/obsidian-dating-provides-proof-for-naval-trade-in-the-aegean-during-the-late-pleistocene-early-holocene-13-10000-ybp/ (quoting Laskaris et al. 2011), MEDIUM]
- Papoulia's review puts the Late Pleistocene Franchthi obsidian at "ca. 14,000–11,000 BP" and calls it "unambiguous evidence of marine crossings." [https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/28488?lang=en, HIGH]
- The Dartmouth site: "obsidian from Melos appears well before the end of the Paleolithic period (ca. 10,900 b.c.)" (uncal.). [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-1/, HIGH]
- **Popular sources give "13,000 BC."** [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franchthi_Cave, MEDIUM] That is at the optimistic end; the safest phrasing is "by the 11th millennium BC, possibly a little earlier," which supports the requester's "~11,000 BC."
- Whether Palaeolithic humans reached Crete/Gavdos much earlier (claims of c. 130,000–70,000 BP from Plakias) remains "controversial" with "no consensus." [https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/28488?lang=en, HIGH]

## 2. Cave of the Cyclops, Youra (Gioura), Northern Sporades

### Where it is
- Youra/Gioura is an uninhabited island of the Northern Sporades (Alonnisos municipality, Thessaly), c. 32 km (20 miles) from Alonnisos; the Cave of the Cyclops is the largest cave in the Sporades. Its neighbours are Pelagos/Kyra Panagia to the (south-)west, Psathoura to the north and Piperi to the east. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gioura, MEDIUM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclops_Cave_(Youra), MEDIUM]
- Kyra Panagia "is also known by the name of Pelagos and rarely Pelagonisi"; Gioura lies to its north-east. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyra_Panagia, MEDIUM] — consistent with the 1683 map's "Pelagnisi."
- Both islands are in Zone A of the National Marine Park of Alonnisos–Northern Sporades (founded 16 May 1992; c. 2,260 km², the largest marine protected area in Europe; created chiefly for the monk seal *Monachus monachus*). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonnisos_Marine_Park, MEDIUM]

### Excavation and dating
- Excavated **1992–1996** by **Adamantios Sampson** (then Ephor/Inspector of Antiquities). Primary report: Sampson, A., "The Neolithic and Mesolithic occupation of the cave of Cyclope, Youra, Alonnessos, Greece," *Annual of the British School at Athens* 93 (1998) 1–22. [https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-of-the-british-school-at-athens/article/abs/neolithic-and-mesolithic-occupation-of-the-cave-of-cyclope-youra-alonnessos-greece/862E05B7A5BC0208BC2C313D389BACD0, HIGH]
- The 1998 abstract: most deposits are Mesolithic, dated roughly **8500–6800 BC**, with ash hearths; "the quantity of fish bones and shells was immense in the Mesolithic levels"; the layers held "a variety of bone hooks" indicating "high dependance on sea resources"; and the cave was then "the earliest site in the Aegean Sea." [same URL, HIGH]
- Final publication: Sampson, A. (ed.), *The Cave of the Cyclops: Mesolithic and Neolithic Networks in the Northern Aegean, Greece*, vol. I (2008) and vol. II, *Bone Tool Industries, Dietary Resources and the Paleoenvironment, and Archaeometrical Studies* (INSTAP Academic Press, Prehistory Monographs 31, 2011). Vol. II contains: A. Moundrea-Agrafioti, "The Mesolithic and Neolithic Bone Implements"; K. Trantalidou, "From Mesolithic Fishermen and Bird Hunters to Neolithic Goat Herders"; **Judith Powell, "Non-Vertebral Fish Bones"**; **Dimitra Mylona, "Fish Vertebrae"**; Y. Facorellis, "Sequential Radiocarbon Dating and Calculation of the Marine Reservoir Effect." [front matter PDF: https://instappress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/01/YII_Frontmatter.pdf, HIGH]
- Facorellis calculated a regional marine reservoir offset of 165 ± 46 years for the northern Aegean from the cave's paired samples. [AJA review by S. E. Allen, *AJA* 118.1 (2014): https://ajaonline.org/book-review/1721/, HIGH]

### The fish-hooks
- The Mesolithic bone-tool assemblage includes "fish hooks of various sizes and shapes, ranging from U-shaped to bipointed types" (Moundrea-Agrafioti 2011). [https://novoscriptorium.com/2019/08/13/… (quoting the 2011 chapter), MEDIUM]
- Mylona's 2014 review describes the Youra hooks as "made of bone and antler" in "two shapes: the bi-pointed gorges and the classic curved shape that survives to date." [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/fish-shellfish-fishermen-prehistoric-aegean/, HIGH]
- The vol. II figures catalogue hooks numbered BH2 through BH35, i.e. roughly three dozen hooks and hook preforms, tabulated by stratum, length and width (Tables 1.8–1.9; Figs 1.5–1.11). [https://instappress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/01/YII_Frontmatter.pdf, HIGH for the numbering; the exact count is not in the front matter — MEDIUM]
- A reviewer stresses the "large assemblage of bone fishhooks" and "their relative abundance as compared with other sites." [https://ajaonline.org/book-review/1721/, HIGH]
- Mylona 2014: fishing "became refined during the late Mesolithic (ca. 6800–6000 BC)" as shown by the stratified hooks. [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/fish-shellfish-fishermen-prehistoric-aegean/, HIGH] Note the slight tension with Sampson's 1998 bracket of 8500–6800 BC; the layers span both.

### What they caught
- Mylona 2014: "The Mesolithic fishermen of Youra exploited two major fish sources: the plentiful migratory fish (i.e., Scombridae and Carangidae) that appeared only seasonally, and the coastal fish available on a year-round basis (e.g., Sparidae, Serranidae, Scorpaenidae)." [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/fish-shellfish-fishermen-prehistoric-aegean/, HIGH]
- Powell's chapter measures grouper (*Epinephelus* sp.), saddled bream (*Oblada melanoura*), two-banded bream (*Diplodus vulgaris*), bluespotted seabream (*Pagrus coeruleostictus*), chub mackerel (*Scomber japonicus*) and scorpionfish; Mylona's chapter concludes the vertebra distributions "suggest the systematic processing and preservation of fish." [https://instappress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/01/YII_Frontmatter.pdf (tables), HIGH; https://ajaonline.org/book-review/1721/, HIGH]
- The cave also yielded shearwater (*Calonectris diomedea*) bones, cetacean vertebrae, and — in the Neolithic — early sheep/goat. [https://instappress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/01/YII_Frontmatter.pdf, HIGH]

## 3. Maroulas, Kythnos ("Fermenia/Thermia") — the oldest island village

- Maroulas is an open-air Mesolithic settlement on the east coast of Kythnos, excavated in rescue campaigns from 1996 and again from 2001 by **Adamantios Sampson** with Janusz K. Kozłowski and V. Giannouli. First report: Sampson, Kozłowski & Giannouli, "The Mesolithic settlement at Maroulas, Kythnos," *Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry* 2 (2002). [https://www.maajournal.com/index.php/maa/article/view/26, HIGH (citation); http://adamantiossampson.blogspot.com/2014/05/mesolithic-settlement-at-kythnos.html, MEDIUM]
- Final publication: Sampson, Kaczanowska & Kozłowski (eds), *The Prehistory of the Island of Kythnos (Cyclades, Greece) and the Mesolithic Settlement at Maroulas* (Kraków: Polish Academy of Arts and Sciences / University of the Aegean, 2010). [https://www.aegeussociety.org/new_book/the-prehistory-of-the-island-of-kythnos-cyclades-greece-and-the-mesolithic-settlement-at-maroulas/, HIGH]
- Dating: radiocarbon determinations of 9346 ± 67, 9571 ± 65, 9440 ± 40 and 9420 ± 50 BP place the site in the **9th millennium BC**. [http://adamantiossampson.blogspot.com/2014/05/mesolithic-settlement-at-kythnos.html, MEDIUM] The AMS programme (Facorellis, Damiata, Vardala-Theodorou, Ntinou & Southon, in the 2010 volume) dates the occupation to c. **8926–8406 BC** and computes a local marine reservoir correction ΔR of −238 ± 48 years. [https://www.academia.edu/12303518/…, MEDIUM (abstract via search)] Another summary: "8800–8600 cal BC." [https://novoscriptorium.com/2019/08/13/…, MEDIUM]
- Architecture and burials: 31 circular/ellipsoid stone structures (one c. 3.2 m across) with paved floors, and burials under the floors — 26 in the full count (Sampson), 8 skeletons in the earlier interim account. [https://novoscriptorium.com/2019/08/13/…, MEDIUM; http://adamantiossampson.blogspot.com/2014/05/mesolithic-settlement-at-kythnos.html, MEDIUM] It is "the only Aegean Mesolithic site which has provided domestic remnants and burials." [https://www.maajournal.com/index.php/maa/article/view/26 (abstract via search), MEDIUM]
- Fishing: Mylona (2010, in the Kraków volume) found "fishing was an important activity," focused on "the migratory, seasonal fish, and distinct inshore species." [https://novoscriptorium.com/2019/08/13/…, MEDIUM] The chipped stone is c. 80 % local quartz and 16.8 % Melian obsidian — i.e. boats were moving between Kythnos and Melos in the 9th millennium BC. [same, MEDIUM]

## 4. Saliagos (between Paros "Paro" and Antiparos) — Neolithic tuna, 5th millennium BC

- Saliagos, now an islet in the strait between Paros and Antiparos, was located in 1961 by N. Zapheiropoulos and excavated in **1964–65** by **John D. Evans and Colin Renfrew** (British School at Athens). Report: Evans & Renfrew, *Excavations at Saliagos near Antiparos* (BSA Supplementary Vol. 5, 1968). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliagos, MEDIUM; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/…/1AC9885AFB4FE3A879D25A07B155DF0D, HIGH (citation)]
- Date: sources vary — c. 4300–3700 BC [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-2/, HIGH], c. 4500–3900 BC (Cambridge review excerpt, MEDIUM), 5000–4500 BC [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliagos, MEDIUM]. "Late 5th millennium BC" is safe.
- **The tuna:** "Large numbers of fish bones were found, of which 97 % of the identifiable pieces belonged to tuna, often of very large size." [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-2/, HIGH] The fish-bone appendix is J. M. Renfrew, P. H. Greenwood & P. J. Whitehead, "Appendix VIII: The fish-bones," in Evans & Renfrew 1968. [search excerpt, MEDIUM] A recent synthesis gives the figures as 2,920 fish bones, 97 % *Thunnus thynnus* and/or *T. alalunga* (bluefin and/or albacore). [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/oa.2798 (Fishing together, fishing on its own — Alepotrypa/Aegean traditions; via search excerpt, article itself not accessible), MEDIUM]
- **How they caught them:** "no fish-hooks were identified among the artifacts of bone or stone and nets are unlikely to have been used to catch fish of this size," so the excavators proposed that the site's characteristic tanged (and tanged-and-barbed) obsidian points were used to spear tuna. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-2/, HIGH] The chipped stone is entirely Melian obsidian; over 25,000 obsidian pieces were recovered. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliagos, MEDIUM]
- The Dartmouth synthesis suggests settlement of the Cyclades "may have been connected with the exploitation of annual tuna runs through the central Aegean." [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-2/, HIGH]

## 5. Melos ("Milo"): obsidian, Phylakopi and the Flying-Fish fresco

- Melos had the two obsidian sources that supplied most of the prehistoric Aegean "first exploited by Upper Palaeolithic populations of the Greek mainland, thereafter circulating throughout the Aegean." [Laskaris et al. 2011 via search excerpt, MEDIUM] Melian tools travelled to the coast and interior of Asia Minor, Macedonia and Crete. [https://cycladic.gr/en/essay/i-thalassia-epikoinonia-eborio-stis-k/, MEDIUM]
- **Phylakopi**, on the north coast, was dug by the British School at Athens in **1896–99** (field director **Duncan Mackenzie**; published by Atkinson, Bosanquet, Edgar et al., *Excavations at Phylakopi in Melos*, 1904), again in 1911, and by **Colin Renfrew in 1974–77** (BSA Supplementary Vol. 42, 2007). Blegen called the 1896–99 dig "the first really serious effort to understand stratification, the first really good excavation in Greece." [https://www.aegeussociety.org/new_book/phylakopi-melos-1896-99-the-finds-in-the-national-archaeological-museum-athens/, HIGH; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylakopi, MEDIUM]
- Phases: Phylakopi I (c. 2300–2000 BC), II (c. 2000–1550 BC), III (c. 1550–1100 BC). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylakopi, MEDIUM]
- **The Flying-Fish fresco:** fragments "had fallen into one of two rooms in the same block which were furnished with central piers" — Cycladic versions of Minoan pillar crypts; dated Late Cycladic I, 16th century BC; **National Archaeological Museum, Athens, inv. no. 5844.** [https://www.worldhistory.org/image/3826/flying-fish-fresco-melos/, MEDIUM; Dartmouth Lesson 18 and ResearchGate figure caption via search, MEDIUM] Definitive study: L. Morgan & M. Cameron, "The Painted Plasters and Their Relation to the Wall Paintings of the Pillar Crypt," in Renfrew (ed.), *Excavations at Phylakopi in Melos 1974–77* (2007) 371–399; and R. L. N. Barber (ed.), *Phylakopi, Melos, 1896–99: The Finds in the National Archaeological Museum, Athens* (BSA Suppl. 53, 2024), which reconstructs the Pillar Crypt decoration with watercolour visualisations. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-17-narrative/lesson-17-bibliography/the-frescoes-of-akrotiri-ayia-irini-and-phylakopi/, HIGH; https://www.aegeussociety.org/new_book/phylakopi-melos-1896-99-…, HIGH]
- A Minoan-period synthesis lists "flying fish" among sea creatures shown in Aegean art specifically citing Phylakopi. [Castleden, *Minoans: Life in Bronze Age Crete* (Routledge 1993), via https://erenow.org/ancient/LifeinBronzeAgeCrete/17.php, MEDIUM]
- Beware commercial print sites captioning the fresco "2500 BC" — that is a millennium too early. [e.g. meisterdrucke listing, LOW]

## 6. Akrotiri, Thera ("St. Erini") — the fishermen, the flotilla, and the eruption

### The frescoes
- Akrotiri was excavated from **1967** by **Spyridon Marinatos** (to his death in 1974), then by Christos Doumas. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Paintings_of_Thera, MEDIUM; https://popular-archaeology.com/article/a-pictorial-the-masters-of-akrotiri/, MEDIUM]
- **The Fishermen:** two wall paintings from **Room 5 of the West House** each show a nude youth with partly shaved head holding bunches of fish strung on yellow cord; they face an offering table found in the room, so many read the scene as ritual offering rather than a market catch. [https://www.worldhistory.org/article/673/akrotiri-frescoes/, MEDIUM; https://therafoundation.org/akrotiri-frescoes/akrotiri-fisherman-fresco, MEDIUM]
- Height: 147 cm [https://therafoundation.org/…/akrotiri-fisherman-fresco; https://www.santorini.net/…, MEDIUM] versus 1.10 m in a Commons file description [https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fresco_of_a_fisherman,_Akrotiri,_Greece.jpg, LOW]. Date: c. 1650–1550 BC (commonly "17th century BC"). [Thera Foundation, MEDIUM]
- **Where it is now:** the best-known Fisherman was transferred to the **Museum of Prehistoric Thera, Fira**, and entered its permanent exhibition on **25 June 2015**. [https://therafoundation.org/akrotiri-frescoes/akrotiri-fisherman-fresco, MEDIUM; https://www.santorini.net/the-famous-fisherman-fresco-is-now-exhibited-in-the-museum-of-prehistoric-thera/, MEDIUM] Older references (and World History Encyclopedia) still say National Archaeological Museum, Athens. [https://www.worldhistory.org/image/2442/fisherman-fresco-akrotiri/, MEDIUM]
- **Species identification — checked:** the fish are most commonly identified as **dolphinfish / dorado / mahi-mahi, *Coryphaena hippurus*** (a fish, not a dolphin). This ID is given in the Wikimedia Commons description [https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fresco_of_a_fisherman,_Akrotiri,_Greece.jpg, MEDIUM], in Commons' catalogue titles for the panels ("Five dolphinfish," "Seven dolphinfish," "Three amberjacks") [https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Fisherman_fresco_(Akrotiri), MEDIUM], and in popular fishing sources [https://mare.it/en/mediterranean-fauna/the-wonderful-colors-of-the-jumping-dolphinfish, LOW]. The specialist treatments are **P. S. Economidis, "The 'Little Fisherman' and the Fish He Holds,"** and **D. Mylona, "The 'Fisherman' Frescoes in the Light of the Fish Bone Evidence,"** both in S. Sherratt (ed.), *The Wall Paintings of Thera: Proceedings of the First International Symposium* (Athens 2000) II: 555–560 and 561–567. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-17-narrative/lesson-17-bibliography/the-frescoes-of-akrotiri-ayia-irini-and-phylakopi/, HIGH for the citations] I could not read those two papers, so treat "dolphinfish" as the standard but not the only proposed identification; note that amberjack is also mentioned for one bunch.
- Fish *bones* from Akrotiri: Mylona's study of processed fish at Akrotiri identified an assemblage of red porgy / common sea bream (*Pagrus pagrus*) and shows fish preservation "was an elaborate process." [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/fish-shellfish-fishermen-prehistoric-aegean/, HIGH; https://www.academia.edu/79800950/…, MEDIUM] A bundled fishing net has also been found preserved at Akrotiri. [Mylona, *Kentro* 19 (2016), https://instapstudycenter.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Vol-19-Kentro-2016-Fall.pdf, HIGH]
- **The Flotilla / Ship Procession (Miniature Frieze):** c. 6 m long, on the south(-west) wall of Room 5, West House; "eight large ships and three smaller vessels, all powered by oarsmen" travel between two towns; "dolphins very similar to those from Knossos leap between the ships"; interpretations range from a real voyage to a nautical festival. [https://www.worldhistory.org/article/673/akrotiri-frescoes/, MEDIUM; https://therafoundation.org/akrotiri-frescoes/ship-procession-fresco, MEDIUM] Key studies: L. Morgan (Brown), "The Ship Procession in the Miniature Fresco," *TAW* II.1 (1978) 629–644; P. M. Warren, *JHS* 99 (1979) 115–129; T. F. Strasser, *JMA* 23 (2010) 3–26. [Dartmouth bibliography, HIGH] I did not find a source confirming fishing *activity* within the Flotilla scene itself — the dolphins are the marine-life element.

### The eruption date controversy
- The eruption was VEI 7, with the latest marine-sediment estimates of 28–41 km³ dense-rock equivalent. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_eruption, MEDIUM]
- **Archaeological/historical date:** Late Minoan IA pottery synchronisms with Egypt's early 18th Dynasty put the eruption c. 1570–1500 BC [https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/08/180815141326.htm, HIGH] or c. 1550–1480 BC [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_eruption, MEDIUM]; some argue for exactly 1524 BC. [Pearson et al. 2023, HIGH]
- **Radiocarbon:** Friedrich et al., "Santorini Eruption Radiocarbon Dated to 1627–1600 B.C.," *Science* 312 (2006) 548 — an olive branch buried alive in the tephra, 72 rings wiggle-matched, 1627–1600 BC at 95.4 %. [https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1125087, HIGH] Its reliability was challenged because olives may not form clear annual rings. [https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-29392-9, HIGH]
- **Convergence:** Pearson et al., "Annual radiocarbon record indicates 16th century BCE date for the Thera eruption," *Science Advances* 4 (2018) eaar8241, re-measured the calibration curve from 285 annual tree rings and widened the radiocarbon window to 1600–1525 BC, overlapping the archaeological range. [https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/08/180815141326.htm, HIGH] Pearson et al., *PNAS* 117 (2020), flagged a tree-ring chemical anomaly c. 1560 BC as a possible Thera signal; Manning replied with methodological objections. [https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1917445117; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7414091/, HIGH]
- **Latest:** Pearson, Sbonias, Tzachili & Heaton, "Olive shrub buried on Therasia supports a mid-16th century BCE date for the Thera eruption," *Scientific Reports* 13: 6994 (2023). Re-modelling the 2006 olive branch with IntCal20 gives death "either between 1621 and 1598 BCE (23.2 %) or between 1593 and 1540 BCE (72.3 %)"; the new Therasia shrub gives 1610–1510 BCE (or 1602–1502 BCE with a regional offset) at 95.4 %, with most probability in the mid-to-late 16th century. Candidate ice-core sulfate spikes are 1611, 1562–1555 and c. 1538 BCE; the authors regard 1611 as "marginalised" by their results and favour 1562–1555 or c. 1538. [PDF: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/199956/13/…pdf, HIGH]
- A 2025 study radiocarbon-dating Egyptian 17th–early 18th Dynasty museum objects is titled "The Minoan Thera eruption predates Pharaoh Ahmose"; I could not read it. [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12422448/, LOW]
- **Bottom line for the reader:** "c. 1600 BC" (radiocarbon, older view) versus "c. 1500 BC" (pottery/Egypt) has narrowed to a debate within the **16th century BC**, most likely between c. 1560 and 1540 BC, still unresolved.

## 7. Minoan Crete ("Candie")

### Marine Style pottery and the Palaikastro octopus flask
- The Marine Style belongs to **Late Minoan IB, c. 1500–1450 BC**: whole vessel surfaces covered with octopus, argonauts, fish and dolphins amid rocks, seaweed and sponges; it was "the last purely Minoan style" before the LM IB destructions. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_pottery, MEDIUM]
- **The Palaikastro flask** (Heraklion Archaeological Museum; the museum's catalogue lists it as "Flask with octopus and seabed in the Marine Style," inv. Π 3383): a wheel-made pilgrim flask 27 cm high, made from two shallow plates joined leather-hard, painted with an octopus "swimming diagonally, with tentacles extended out to the full perimeter" among sea urchins, coral and triton shells; c. 1500 BC. [https://ca.heraklionmuseum.gr/ca/pawtucket/index.php/Detail/objects/238, MEDIUM (site certificate blocked full read); https://human.libretexts.org/…/2.9.2.06:_Octopus_vase (Smarthistory, Dr Senta German), HIGH] The museum dates it 1500–1450 BC and describes tentacles covering the whole surface with sea urchins, tritons and seaweed-covered rocks between them. [https://heraklionmuseum.gr/en/exhibit/marine-style-flask/ (via search excerpt), MEDIUM] Some attribute it to a hand dubbed the "Marine Style Master" working at Palaikastro. [Smarthistory via search excerpt, MEDIUM]

### Hooks, sinkers and nets
- At **Gournia** (dug by Harriet Boyd Hawes, 1901–04) "well-made metal fish-hooks were found … together with a lead sinker for a fishing line and some stones which could have been used for weighting nets" — line and net fishing. A bronze double-headed spear from Ayios Onouphrios may be a fish spear. [Castleden 1993 via https://erenow.org/ancient/LifeinBronzeAgeCrete/17.php, MEDIUM]
- The Metropolitan Museum holds a Minoan bronze fish hook dated Early Minoan III–Late Minoan I. [https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/251558, MEDIUM — page repeatedly rate-limited; title only]
- **Mochlos (LM IB, c. 1500–1450 BC):** Mylona, "On Fish Bones, Seashells, Fishermen, and Seaside Living at Late Minoan IB Mochlos," *Kentro* 19 (2016) 1–5: the LM IB strata produced **852 fish bones and 5,865 marine molluscs**; fish from at least 13 families, from under 8–10 cm (damselfish, picarels) to nearly 1 m (grouper *Epinephelus*, dentex *Dentex dentex*). Most are inshore shoal fish (picarels, bogues, damselfish, combers, juvenile parrotfish and breams) catchable with cast nets from shore or boat; what "sets the LM IB fish assemblage from Mochlos apart" is the quantity of large bottom fish — groupers, dentex, red porgy *Pagrus pagrus*, striped seabream *Lithognathus mormyrus*, pandora *Pagellus erythrinus*, parrotfish *Sparisoma cretense*, mullets — taken with "harpoons or hooks and lines" in deeper water. Gear: a bronze fishhook from an LM IB floor, another bronze hook, perforated stone weights (Soles 2004); a pumice float type is noted; crushed *Hexaplex trunculus* from a purple-dye workshop was reused as building fill. [https://instapstudycenter.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Vol-19-Kentro-2016-Fall.pdf, HIGH]
- The seal-stone image of "a muscular fisherman proudly holding a skaros [parrotfish] in one hand and an octopus in the other" (chalcedony intaglio) is the classic Minoan fisherman portrait; parrotfish (*skaros*) also appears on Knossos gold work. [Castleden via erenow, MEDIUM]

### Kommos
- Fish remains from the Minoan town were published by **Mark Rose, "Fish Remains from Minoan Kommos,"** in J. W. & M. C. Shaw (eds), *Kommos I: The Kommos Region and Houses of the Minoan Town*, Part 1 (Princeton 1995) 204–239. [search excerpt, MEDIUM] Rose's dissertation is *With Line and Glittering Bronze Hook: Fishing in the Aegean Bronze Age* (Indiana University, 1994). [cited in Mylona 2016, https://instapstudycenter.net/…Vol-19-Kentro-2016-Fall.pdf, HIGH]
- Species recorded at Kommos include angel shark (*Squatina squatina*), eel (*Anguilla anguilla*), garfish (*Belone belone*), sea bass (*Dicentrarchus labrax*), Sparidae (*Diplodus*, *Pagellus erythrinus*), picarel, parrotfish, Mugilidae, sole (*Solea solea*), Sciaenidae and Labridae. [Rose, "The Fish Remains," in *Kommos IV: The Greek Sanctuary* (2000), PDF https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/da89e455-5c1d-4616-8434-61a1b1aca206/content, HIGH — note this chapter concerns the Iron Age sanctuary but lists the site's taxa]
- **A trap for the unwary:** three bronze hooks from Kommos "were originally published as Minoan, presumably because they are bronze," but actually came from Hellenistic, Geometric/Archaic and 1st-century AD findspots. [same Kommos IV PDF, HIGH] Bronze does not mean Bronze Age.
- Lead line/net weights — "simple strips of lead folded lengthwise over a line or edge of a net" — are "known from Middle Bronze Age through Byzantine sites in the eastern Mediterranean." [same PDF, HIGH] The most recent survey of Aegean lead net sinkers: S. Privitera, "Fishermen, Sailors, or Boat Owners? Lead Fishnet Sinkers, Fishing, and Society in the Late Bronze Age III Aegean," *AJA* 130.3 (2026) 325–353. [https://ajaonline.org/article/fishermen-sailors-or-boat-owners/, HIGH (abstract)]

### Purple dye (murex)
- In **1903 R. C. Bosanquet** found masses of murex at a Middle Minoan site on **Kouphonisi** (ancient Leuke), off south-east Crete, and in **1904** purple-shell deposits at **Palaikastro**; Stieglitz's 1981 survey found "a large surface deposit of murex remains on the southern slopes of the Kastri" at Palaikastro. Species: *Hexaplex (Murex) trunculus* and *Bolinus (Murex) brandaris*. These deposits date to the Middle Minoan period, c. 2000–1600 BC. [https://www.keftiu.com/magazine/the-minoan-origin-of-tyrian-purple/, MEDIUM; the underlying paper is R. R. Stieglitz, "The Minoan Origin of Tyrian Purple," *Biblical Archaeologist* 57 (1994), https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261818482_The_Minoan_Origin_of_Tyrian_Purple, MEDIUM]
- At **Kommos**, Middle Minoan II/III levels produced "industrial remains of a Murex dyeing installation," with crushed murex in contemporary levels at Palaikastro and Knossos. [Ruscillo/Reese, "Faunal Remains and Murex Dye Production," in *Kommos V*, via search excerpt, MEDIUM]
- Mylona's overview dates the start of Aegean purple production to "ca 1800 BC onwards." [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/fish-shellfish-fishermen-prehistoric-aegean/, HIGH]
- Murex residues have since been chemically identified in vessels at Alatzomouri-Pefka (east Crete): Pareja, Betancourt, Apostolakou, Brogan & Koh, "Aegean Dyes," *Expedition* 58.3 (2017). [https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/aegean-dyes/, HIGH]

## 8. Ayia Irini, Kea ("Zea")

- Ayia Irini sits on a small peninsula at the head of Kea's main bay (Vourkari); first settled at the start of the Early Bronze Age (early 3rd millennium BC) and occupied, with gaps, through the Late Bronze Age; excavated by **John L. Caskey** (University of Cincinnati) in the 1960s–70s; the periods are numbered I–VIII and published in the *Keos* series (e.g. Cummer & Schofield, *Keos III: House A*; Schofield, *Keos X: The Western Sector*; Abell, *Keos XII: Area B*, 2021). [https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2023/2023.05.20/, HIGH; https://www.greeka.com/cyclades/kea/sightseeing/prehistoric-settlement-agia-irini/, LOW]
- Fortification walls, a temple, and fresco fragments (contemporary with Akrotiri's but far more fragmentary) are among the finds. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-17-narrative/, HIGH; https://www.greeka.com/…, LOW]
- Lead and litharge were recovered at Ayia Irini, and lead provenance has been studied — relevant because lead is the material of Bronze Age net sinkers. [https://www.academia.edu/84565075/The_Provenance_of_Lead_Used_at_Ayia_Irini_Keos, MEDIUM (title only)]
- **Fishing evidence:** popular guides say the inhabitants were "farmers and fishermen dealing with metallurgy" [https://www.greeka.com/…, LOW]. The Period V fauna was studied by J. Coy (*Keos V*), and C. Gamble's "Surplus and Self-Sufficiency in the Cycladic Subsistence Economy" (in Davis & Cherry eds, *Papers in Cycladic Prehistory*, 1979) treats Cycladic subsistence including Phylakopi fauna. [Dartmouth Lesson 17 bibliography, HIGH for citations] **I found no published fish-bone counts or fishing-gear catalogue for Ayia Irini** in accessible sources — see §12.

## 9. Poliochni, Lemnos ("Stalimene")

- Poliochni lies on the east coast of Lemnos "overlooking a bay protected from the winds by a rocky promontory and forming a natural harbour," on a rise near the cove of Vroskopos between two rivers; the tell is over 9 m deep. [http://www.luigibernabobrea.it/luoghi/poliochni_e.html, MEDIUM; https://www.greeknewsagenda.gr/poliochne-on-the-island-of-lemnos-…, MEDIUM]
- Excavated by the **Italian Archaeological School at Athens**: **Alessandro Della Seta** 1930/31–1936 (about two-thirds of the settlement), then **Luigi Bernabò Brea** in 1951–53, 1956 and 1960; monographs *Poliochni I* (1964) and *II* (1976). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poliochne, MEDIUM; http://www.luigibernabobrea.it/luoghi/poliochni_e.html, MEDIUM]
- Bernabò Brea's colour-coded phases: Black (Early Bronze I, stone and bone tools only, no metal), Blue (walled, first metal), Green, Red (bronze hoard), Yellow (fortified town "considerably larger than the contemporary walled citadel of Troy," gold jewellery hoard found 1956), Brown, Mycenaean. [https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-7-narrative/, HIGH] Absolute dates offered by the Greek Ministry-affiliated summary: Blue 3200–2700, Green 2700–2400, Red 2400–2200, Yellow 2200–2100 BC; population c. 800–1,000 (Blue) rising to c. 1,500 (Green); enclosure wall 130 m long, up to 4.5 m high. [https://www.greeknewsagenda.gr/…, MEDIUM] (Wikipedia's "c. 2500–2000 BC" is looser; Dartmouth stresses the dates are debated relative to Troy I–V. MEDIUM)
- Economy: "Residents engaged in farming, fishing, textile-making, the manufacture of stone tools and weapons, and metalworking." [https://www.greeknewsagenda.gr/…, MEDIUM] **I could not locate a published fish-bone study or hook/sinker catalogue for Poliochni** — see §12.

## 10. What people caught, and with what

### Species (bone evidence, by period)
- **Mesolithic (Franchthi, Youra, Maroulas):** inshore breams first, then barracuda, then tuna at Franchthi [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21371735/, HIGH]; at Youra migratory Scombridae (mackerels/tunas) and Carangidae (jacks) seasonally plus year-round Sparidae (breams), Serranidae (groupers/combers) and Scorpaenidae (scorpionfish) [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/…, HIGH].
- **Neolithic:** groupers, scorpionfish, John Dory, wrasses, sea breams, grey mullets, mackerels, pelamids (bonito), little tunnies and tunas at coastal sites; tuna overwhelmingly at Saliagos. [https://www.archaeology.wiki/blog/2014/11/24/…, HIGH; https://sites.dartmouth.edu/aegean-prehistory/lessons/lesson-2/, HIGH]
- **Bronze Age (Mylona's synthesis, *Journal of Biological Research–Thessaloniki* 21:2, 2014):** "people all along the Aegean caught and ate mostly small inshore fish. In the Southern Aegean the picarels and bogues were the most common species, with small annular sea breams, combers, damsel fish, small pandoras and striped sea bream as well as groupers, scorpion fishes, leer fish, barracudas, sting rays and sharks." [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2241-5793-21-2 (via search excerpt; full text behind cookie wall), MEDIUM-HIGH] Mochlos adds large grouper, dentex, red porgy and parrotfish [Kentro 19, HIGH]; Akrotiri adds processed red porgy [archaeology.wiki, HIGH].
- Bluefin tuna bones occur at Aegean sites across 9000–3200 BC. [https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/79/2/247/6511216, HIGH]

### Gear
- **Bone/antler hooks** (U-shaped and bipointed gorges): Mesolithic Youra, 9th–7th millennium BC. [archaeology.wiki, HIGH; BSA 93, HIGH]
- **Obsidian tanged points** as fish spears/harpoons: Saliagos, 5th millennium BC (inferred; no hooks found). [Dartmouth Lesson 2, HIGH]
- **Bronze hooks** (barbed and unbarbed, gapes c. 1.5 cm at Kommos in later periods): Gournia, Mochlos (LM IB floor), Met Museum example EM III–LM I. [erenow/Castleden, MEDIUM; Kentro 19, HIGH; Kommos IV, HIGH]
- **Lead sinkers** (folded strips) from the Middle Bronze Age on; **pierced stone weights**; **pumice floats**; **nets** (a bundled net survives at Akrotiri); small cast nets and larger seines, baited baskets and fixed traps are inferred. [Kommos IV PDF, HIGH; Kentro 19, HIGH; archaeology.wiki, HIGH]
- The Kommos chapter warns that hook size is a poor guide to target size because many Aegean fish have small mouths for their body (Powell 1996: 127–28). [Kommos IV PDF, HIGH]

### Boats
- No Mesolithic or Neolithic boat survives; the Melos obsidian at Franchthi (11th millennium BC), on Kythnos (9th millennium) and on Youra is the proof of seagoing craft. [§1–3 above]
- **Early Cycladic "frying pans" from Chalandriani, Syros ("Sira"):** the Chalandriani cemetery was excavated by **Christos Tsountas in 1898–99** (540 graves in a year; more than 600 now known — the largest Early Cycladic cemetery), dated Early Cycladic II, c. 2700–2300/2200 BC. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalandriani, MEDIUM; https://cyclades.culture.gov.gr/en/location/the-prehistoric-and-classical-antiquities-of-syros/, HIGH] Syros-type frying pans carry stamped spirals and incised longboats far more often than those from other islands. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalandriani, MEDIUM]
- **NAM 4974**, from Chalandriani grave 74 (found 1889/90; published by Tsountas, *Kykladika* II, 1899), c. 2500 BC: an incised longboat with a high prow bearing "a fish symbol and two banners," oars shown as slanting lines above and below the hull; National Archaeological Museum, Athens. Standard study: J. E. Coleman, "'Frying Pans' of the Early Bronze Age Aegean," *AJA* 89 (1985). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frying_pan_(NAMA_4974), MEDIUM] The Museum of Cycladic Art describes the typical craft as "paddled longboats with a high bow (or stern, according to others), usually crowned by a fish," and cites estimates of a crew of 15–20, about a ton of cargo, and roughly two weeks to cross most of the Aegean. [https://cycladic.gr/en/essay/i-thalassia-epikoinonia-eborio-stis-k/, MEDIUM] Note the "high prow with fish" is read by some as the stern.
- **Kastri**, the fortified hilltop just north of Chalandriani, is dated by the Ephorate of Antiquities of the Cyclades to Early Cycladic IIIA (Kastri group), 2300–2000 BC; popular sites give 2800–2300 BC. [https://cyclades.culture.gov.gr/en/location/the-prehistoric-and-classical-antiquities-of-syros/, HIGH; contrast search excerpts, LOW] It shows Trojan-style metalwork and pottery. [Dartmouth Lesson 7, HIGH]
- **Theran ships (c. 1600 BC):** the Flotilla frieze shows oared ships with cabins, rigging and steering oars — the fullest picture of Aegean Bronze Age vessels before the Uluburun wreck. [§6]

## 12. Things I could not verify

1. **Ayia Irini (Kea) fishing evidence.** I found no accessible fish-bone quantification or catalogue of hooks/sinkers for the site. J. Coy's fauna study (*Keos V*) and the *Keos III* House A small finds would be the places to look. The "farmers and fishermen" line rests on a tourist site only.
2. **Poliochni (Lemnos) fishing evidence.** Same problem: the Italian monographs (Bernabò Brea 1964, 1976) are not online in searchable form; the only accessible statement is a generic "farming, fishing, textile-making" summary.
3. **The species on the Akrotiri Fishermen frescoes at scholarly level.** "Dolphinfish (*Coryphaena hippurus*)" is the standard identification in museum-adjacent and popular sources, and "amberjack" is applied to one bunch, but I could not open Economidis 2000 or Mylona 2000 to confirm what the ichthyologist and the zooarchaeologist actually concluded. Some older popular texts say "mackerel"; I found no scholarly source for that.
4. **Exact number of bone hooks from the Cave of the Cyclops.** The catalogue numbering (BH2–BH35) and "several dozen" are all I could confirm; the precise total is in Moundrea-Agrafioti's 2011 chapter, which I could not read.
5. **Saliagos "2,920 bones / 97 % tuna."** The 97 % figure is solid (Dartmouth, citing the 1968 report); the 2,920 count comes from a search excerpt of a 2019 *International Journal of Osteoarchaeology* paper I could not open.
6. **Whether fishing is depicted in the Flotilla fresco.** Dolphins yes; I found no source describing fishermen or fishing gear within the Ship Procession itself.
7. **Kommos Middle Minoan murex installation details.** The MM II/III dye-installation statement comes from a search excerpt of Ruscillo's *Kommos V* chapter, not from the chapter itself.
8. **The 28-oar count on a Syros frying pan.** A National Archaeological Museum "artefact of the month" page apparently describes a ship "with 28 oars"; the page was inaccessible (HTTP 403), so the count is unverified.
9. **The Met's Minoan bronze fish hook.** The object page was rate-limited on every attempt; only the search-result title (EM III–LM I) is available.
10. **Franchthi obsidian "13,000 BC."** This popular figure could not be traced to a primary statement; the primary literature supports the 11th millennium cal BC (Perlès) or "c. 14,000–11,000 BP" (Papoulia) — I recommend the more conservative "by c. 11,000 BC."
11. **Tel Kabri, Kythera, Mallia murex, and Knossos fish-in-cooking-pot details** were noted in passing in secondary sources but not checked.
12. **The 1683 map name equivalences** (Milo, St. Erini, Candie, Zea, Stalimene, Sira, Paro, Fermenia/Thermia, Pelagnisi) were supplied by the requester; I verified only Pelagos/Pelagonisi = Kyra Panagia.
