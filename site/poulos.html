Poulos

The ending -poulos means son of, or descendant of. It comes into Greek in the Byzantine centuries, most likely from the Latin pullus, a young animal, a chick or a foal. Greek already had its own word polos, a foal, so the two probably propped each other up. The derivation is not perfectly clean and anyone who tells you it is has tidied it.1

What matters more is where it is from. -poulos is the Peloponnesian ending. Other regions took other ones, -akis in Crete, -idis among the Pontic Greeks, and a Greek can usually place a stranger's family by the last three syllables of their name.2

So the name points at a stretch of country, and that country is on the map you are holding. It is at the bottom left, under the word Moree, which is what Venetian pilots called the Peloponnese. It is drawn here. The oldest fish bones in this whole history came out of a cave on its coast.3

Standing alone

On its own, Poulos is a shortened name. Oxford's dictionary of American family names defines it that way: a longer -poulos patronymic with the front of it dropped.4 Greek surnames get long, and Greeks in America shortened them constantly.

Which longer name is not recoverable. The surviving half carries no information about the half that went. Papadopoulos, son of the priest, is the most common surname in Greece, which makes it the most likely guess and still only a guess.5 Anyone who hands you a specific original is inventing it.

The name and the sea

There is no documented connection between -poulos families and fishing. None. It is a naming habit of a region, not a trade, and the honest answer to whether the name means fishermen is no.

What is documented sits next to it. The Peloponnese is coast on three sides. The Mani, its middle finger of rock, ran a long tradition of seafaring and piracy.6 And when Greeks built the American sponge fishery at Tarpon Springs in Florida from about 1905, the divers came mostly from the Dodecanese, with -akis and -is names rather than -poulos ones. The man who organised it, John Cocoris, was born in the Peloponnese.7

How the names were given

Hereditary surnames only fixed in Greece in the 1820s and 1830s, alongside the modern state.8 Before that a man was his father's son and that was the whole of it. The older custom outlasted the paperwork: the first son is named for his father's father, so names skip a generation and come back, which is why the same few turn up over and over in one family.9
