Overview

The object is a copperplate engraving about 14 by 10 centimetres, cut for Alain Manesson Mallet's Description de l'Univers and printed in Paris in 1683. It is figure LXXIV of the fourth volume, the European half of the Archipelago. The names on it are Greek names heard by Venetian pilots: Candie for Crete, Moree for the Peloponnese, Negrepont for Euboea.

This site hangs a history of fishing on that sheet. There are 41 stops, each pinned to an island you can tap, running from the first crossings of open water to a trawling ban promised for 2030. Every stop carries its sources. Below is the whole story in fourteen beats, and each one opens the island it belongs to.

The arc

  1. by 11,000 BC

    Somebody crosses open water

    Obsidian from Milos turns up at Franchthi Cave on the Peloponnese, about 150 kilometres away across the sea. No boat has ever been found. The glass is the proof.

    Milos on the map

  2. c. 8900 to 8400 BC

    The oldest village in the islands

    Maroulas on Kythnos: thirty-one round and oval stone structures with paved floors and burials underneath. The fish bones show fishing mattered. One stone tool in six is Melian obsidian, so boats were running.

    Kythnos on the map

  3. late 5th millennium BC

    A village that lived on tuna

    At Saliagos, between Paros and Antiparos, ninety-seven per cent of the identifiable fish bones were tuna, often very large. No hooks were found. The excavators think the tanged obsidian points were for spearing them.

    Paros on the map

  4. c. 2000 to 1450 BC

    Fishing becomes a trade

    Crete is where the gear gets serious: metal hooks at Gournia with a lead line sinker and stones for weighting nets. At Mochlos, 852 fish bones from at least thirteen families, inshore shoals taken in cast nets and big bottom fish taken on hooks in deeper water.

    Crete on the map

  5. c. 1600 to 1540 BC

    The fisherman of Akrotiri

    A naked boy with a half-shaved head holds two bunches of fish strung on yellow cord, painted on a wall on Thera and buried by the eruption that blew the middle out of the island.

    Santorini on the map

  6. c. 700 BC

    Homer will not let his heroes eat fish

    Nobody in the Iliad or the Odyssey eats fish at table, and Plato noticed. But the poet knew the gear: line and gleaming bronze hook, a lead plummet set on an ox-horn guard, the many-meshed net the dead suitors are heaped in.

    Euboea on the map

  7. 600 to 300 BC

    Byzantium, mother-city of the tunny

    Kyzikos struck electrum coins with a tunny beneath the main type for three centuries, the oldest tuna coins in the world. Archestratus sends a friend to Byzantium for tail-cuts of tuna. The city itself lies just beyond the top-right corner of the sheet.

    The Hellespont on the map

  8. 425 to 330 BC

    Athens goes mad for fish

    The word opson meant anything eaten with bread, and it came to mean fish above all. The comic poets built a whole gallery of fishmongers. Antiphanes: one glance at them and I am turned to stone, when I see what a small fish it is for which they charge such a price.

    Athens on the map

  9. c. AD 180

    The manual

    Oppian of Cilicia writes the Halieutica, 3,606 hexameters in five books. The fisherman, he says, must be swift, cunning, daring, keen-sighted, and must love the sea. His four modes, hook, net, weel and trident, were still the four modes of the Aegean in the twentieth century.

    Euboea on the map

  10. 1207 to 1579

    The word on the map was a state

    Three years after the Fourth Crusade broke Byzantium, the Venetian Marco Sanudo took eight galleys and made himself Duke of the Archipelago at Naxos. The Sanudi ruled until 1383 and the Crispi after them, until Selim II deposed the last Latin duke in 1566. The Ottomans took the rest apart piece by piece.

    Naxos on the map

  11. 1676 to 1700

    The sea as it was when the plate was printed

    Ios was inhabited by thieves by profession, so the Turks called it Little Malta, a harbour for most of the corsairs of the Mediterranean. Mallet writes of islands almost deserted because of the frequent insults of the corsairs. Fishing here in 1683 meant fishing within sight of raiders.

    Ios on the map

  12. 1774 to 1821

    The islands build fleets

    The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca let Christian subjects of the Sultan trade under the Russian flag, and the Saronic islands seized it. Hydra had about a hundred and fifty ships by the early nineteenth century. Spetses had sixty ships, twenty-seven hundred seamen and nine hundred guns by 1813.

    Aegina on the map

  13. 1922 to 1923

    The refugees bring the gri-gri

    More than a million people arrived from Asia Minor and raised Greece's population by about a quarter. At Limenaria on Thasos, local history credits refugee fishermen with the gri-gri: a mother boat towing a string of small lamp-boats that scatter at night to draw anchovy and sardine under the light.

    Thasos on the map

  14. 1966 to 2030

    The law catches up, and the catch falls

    Royal Decree 917 of 1966 still shuts the trawlers down inshore from June to September. Official landings fell from 81,920 tonnes in 2019 to 42,101 in 2025. In April 2024 Greece became the first country in Europe to promise a ban on bottom trawling in all its marine protected areas, in national parks by 2026 and everywhere by 2030.

    Athens on the map
